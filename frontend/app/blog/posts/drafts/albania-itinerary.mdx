If you're planning an Albania itinerary, you're in for a treat. Albania is one of Europe's last true hidden gems — and one of its best-value destinations. While the rest of the Mediterranean has become increasingly crowded and expensive, Albania delivers stunning mountain scenery, crystal-clear beaches, and rich history at a fraction of the cost.

This 9-day Albania road trip itinerary is designed to give you the best of both worlds: the rugged peaks and glacial lakes of the Albanian Alps in the North, and the sun-drenched beaches and ancient ruins of the Albanian Riviera in the South. With your own rental car, you can move at your own pace and reach places most package tourists never see.

## Safety in Albania

Albania is generally a very safe country for travelers. The locals are incredibly hospitable and welcoming. As with any destination, use common sense: keep an eye on your belongings in crowded areas and be cautious when driving. The tap water is generally safe for drinking. While we were fine, we did notice some areas where food safety is lacking, mainly hotel buffets letting hot food get cold, so keep an eye out for that.


## Renting a Car in Albania

To truly see Albania, you'll want your own wheels. We rented our car through **Enterprise** and had a great experience. Having a rental car allowed us to navigate the mountain passes of the North and the coastal roads of the South at our own pace. 

If you have a European or English-language driving license, you should not need to get an international driving permit (IDP). We rented with a Canadian license and they didn't require one. It's worth checking with the rental agency before booking.

The local driving standards are likely less good than your home country, make sure you are adequately insured against parking dings and side scratches.

**Tip:** If you're planning on extending your trip to cross the border into Kosovo, Montenegro, or Greece, ensure your rental agreement allows for this. Most rental companies have GPS trackers on their cars and will fine you if you don't pay the cross-border fee up front, or worse, you'll be at the border without the right paperwork and may not be allowed to pass.

## Driving in Albania

Albania has not had cars for all that long. Private ownership of motor vehicles was outlawed during the dictatorship, and even after democracy returned, driving licenses were not common even after people started buying cars. While driving standards have come a long way since my first visit in 2016, it can still be a little hectic. Here are some tips:
- Albanians will often tailgate if they intend to pass. If you feel someone is too close to you, give them some space to pass you.
- Off the highway, and especially in the mountains, the roads get very narrow, and still carry two lanes of traffic. Be prepared to drive slowly and pull over to squeeze past oncoming vehicles.
- Drivers are assertive, and often aggressive, so be on alert. People will often pull out when they don't have right of way, or cause you to brake hard to avoid a collision.
- Oncoming drivers will flash to warn you of a police speed trap - yes this is illegal, yes they still do it.
- Yield and stop signs are mostly ignored. That doesn't mean you should ignore them, just be aware that the locals may drive unpredictably.

But don't let this deter you. We settled into the local rhythm of driving quickly and did not find it stressful.

## Is a 4x4 Required to Drive in Albania?

In a word, no. Most of Albania's roads are actually in very good condition. While they may be narrow, they are well paved with very few nasty potholes. Because many roads are narrow, I would even recommend a smaller car to reduce some stress while driving.

Some exceptions:
- Çorovodë to Poliçan - As of 2026 you need a 4x4 to drive this road. It is not paved and you need a vehicle with high ground clearance and good traction.
- Shköder to Komani lake - As of May 2026 the road is currently being repaved and good progress is being made, but there is still some 20+km of gravel roads. We drove this road in a compact car with low ground clearance and standard tires and had no problems, but it was a surprise.

The road from Shköder to Theth is well paved all the way, you do not need a 4x4 or snow chains outside of the winter months. Yes, the road is narrow and windy, but it is paved and safe to drive.

## North vs South Albania: Which Should You Visit?

One of the most common questions when planning an Albania trip is whether to head North to the mountains or South to the beaches. If you have 9 days, the answer is **both**. 

The North offers dramatic scenery in the Albanian Alps and the serene beauty of Komani Lake. The South is home to the stunning Albanian Riviera — the Ionian coast — and historic towns like Berat and Gjirokaster. This itinerary is designed to give you a taste of both worlds. Because it's not a big country, your longest drive will be 4 hours, so it doesn't eat an entire day to get from one end to the other.

## 9-Day Albania Itinerary: Day-by-Day Breakdown

### Day 1: Tirana to Komani Lake (North)

After arriving in Tirana, pick up your rental car and head North. The drive to Komani Lake takes about 2.5 hours.

- **Stay:** [Borealis Guest House](https://www.booking.com) – A beautiful spot right on the water.
- **Dinner:** **Bar restaurant "Te Kastrioti"** – Highly recommended for an amazing dinner with local flavors (get the grilled sausages and thank us later!).
- **Drinks:** **Lake Side Lounge** – Relax on bean bag chairs by the lake with a drink in hand. There's often a campfire for extra cozy vibes. 

### Day 2: Komani Lake Day Trip

Spend the day exploring the stunning Komani Lake. The ferry ride through the narrow canyons is often compared to the Norwegian fjords and is a highlight of any trip to Albania. You can take a boat tour that visits remote villages or just enjoy the spectacular scenery from the water.

### Day 3: Theth

Drive from Komani Lake to Theth (approx. 3 hours). The drive takes you through a beautiful mountain pass with incredible views of the Prokletije (Accursed Mountains).

- **Stay:** [Guesthouse Prrockaj](https://www.booking.com) – A traditional and welcoming stay in the heart of the village.

### Day 4: Hiking & Blue Eye

Theth is a hiker's paradise. Spend the day exploring the valley. A must-visit is the **Blue Eye (Syri i Kaltër)** of Theth, a stunning natural spring with vibrant blue water. It's a moderate hike from the village and well worth the effort.

We did a moderate 7.5km hike ([AllTrails Link](https://www.alltrails.com/trail/albania/shkoder/red-2a-hardedaj-nenreth-theth)) which gave us some lovely views of the valley.

There is a much harder 18km hike over the Theth - Valbona pass ([AllTrails Link](https://www.alltrails.com/trail/albania/shkoder/peaks-of-the-balkans-theth-valbone)). There are some cafes serving hot meals along the trail, and many people do this as a one way hike, but that's not feasible if you're self-driving.

If you want to see the Blue Eye you have 2 options:
1) There's a gruelling 19km out-and-back hike to see it ([AllTrails Link](https://www.alltrails.com/trail/albania/shkoder/7-the-blue-eye))
2) You can hire a 4x4 taxi to take you there for about $50-70 round trip. There's a 2 hour / 4km hike from the drop-off point to the eye.

- **Stay:** Guesthouse Prrockaj - down in the valley, comfortable beds, cozy, warm log cabin vibes. (We stayed here)
- **Stay:** Thethi Hotel - a more luxurious option, better for warm weather has it as an outdoor pool with an incredible view.

### Day 5: Return to Tirana

Head back to the capital, Tirana (approx. 3.5 hour drive). Use the afternoon to dive into Albania's complex history.

- **Free Walking Tour:** A great way to orient yourself and get a feel for the city's history. We used [Free Tour Tirana](https://www.freetour.com/tirana/welcome-to-tirana-free-walking-tour) and highly recommend it as a first stop.
- **House of Leaves:** A museum dedicated to the secret surveillance of the communist era.
- **Bunk'Art 1:** Located next to the cable car. Massive, five-story underground atomic bunker built for Albania's paranoid dictator that has been converted into an eerie historical museum. 
- **Bunk'Art 2:** Located downtown Tirana. Big bunker converted into art and history museums where you can learn about the horrors of the communist dictatorship. 
- **Cable Car:** Take the Dajti Ekspres cable car for panoramic views of the city, there's a good restaurant at the top serving traditional fare, and even a mini golf course.
- **Skanderbeu Square:** The centerpoint of the city, famously framed by a striking clash of Ottoman, Italian, and Soviet-era architecture. Reborn from its chaotic past into an award-winning eco-plaza, it is paved with stones from across Albania as a powerful symbol of national unity.

**Stay**: Mariott Tirana - we decided to splurge and stayed in (quite possibly) the nicest hotel in the city.

### Day 6: Saranda & the Albanian Riviera

Time to head South to the Albanian Riviera! The drive from Tirana to Saranda takes about 4-5 hours. You'll notice the landscape change dramatically from the mountains of the North to the sparkling Ionian Sea. We stopped along the way to break up the drive, but we'd recommend continuing another hour and making it all the way to Saranda.

Saranda is the Albanian Riviera's vibrant coastal gateway, nestled along a horseshoe bay. Filled with hotels and apartments overlooking the Adriatic, perched on a hillside, walking along the recently renovated promenade really looks and feels a lot like Monaco (just without the casinos, megayachts, and supercars). Seamlessly blending a bustling palm-lined Riviera promenade with ancient history, it serves as a great base for exploring the turquoise beaches of Ksamil and the nearby Roman ruins of Butrint, and the perfect spot for some fresh seafood.

### Day 7: Ksamil, Butrint & the Best of the Riviera

The Albanian Riviera is at its best around Saranda. All of these attractions are about 30-45 minutes drive from the city: 
- **Ksamil Beach:** Known for its turquoise water and small islands you can swim or boat to.
- **Butrint National Archaeological Park:** A UNESCO World Heritage site featuring ruins from Greek, Roman, Byzantine, and Venetian periods.
- **The Blue Eye (Syri i Kaltër)**: A hypnotic natural water spring that bubbles up from an unexplored depth of over 50 meters, featuring vibrant electric-blue water. Lovely lake to walk around.
- **Lëkurësi Castle**: A 16th-century fortress sitting on a hill above Saranda, offering panoramic views of the bay and unforgettable sunsets over Corfu.
- **Mirror Beach (Plazhi i Pasqyrave)**: A dramatic, cliff-backed cove famous for its glittering, reflective waters that consistently ranks among the prettiest spots on the Riviera.

### Day 8: Berat and Gjirokaster

Drive to Berat (approx. 3 hours). On the way, make sure to stop at **Gjirokaster**, to see the beautiful old town, perched on a mountain, with a castle overlooking it. We stayed overnight, but it's small enough that you could see it in 2 hours.

Berat is known as the "City of a Thousand Windows" due to its well-preserved Ottoman architecture. The Mangalem and Gorica Quarters are separated by the Osum river and are just a few minutes walk from one another if you take the Gorica Bridge.

- **Berat Castle (Kalaja)**: A massive 13th-century hilltop fortress containing a living, inhabited neighborhood filled with historic stone houses, small cafés and restaurants, and the renowned Onufri Iconographic Museum. Limited parking (all paid), entrance fee from 9am-5pm.
- **Mangalem Quarter**: The historic Ottoman hillside district featuring narrow cobblestone alleys and densely packed white stone houses that earned Berat its nickname, the "City of a Thousand Windows." Limited parking, consider walking from your hotel.
- **Gorica Quarter**: The traditionally Christian neighborhood sitting across the Osum River, offering a quieter, atmospheric maze of old-world lanes and the best photo angles of Mangalem's stacked windows and the castle above. Limited parking, consider walking from your hotel.
- **Gorica Bridge**: An elegant, multi-arched stone bridge originally built during the Ottoman era that serves as the iconic pedestrian link between the two historic sides of the city.  
- **Optional:** If you have more time, consider an extended stay for a tour to the **Osumi Canyon**. Without a 4x4 you cannot drive to see it.

### Day 9: Return to Tirana

Complete the loop with a 2-hour drive back to Tirana for your flight home.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Albania safe for tourists?**
Albania is remarkably safe for travelers, boasting violent crime rates lower than many Western European capitals and an overall safety index comparable to Canada. Driven by a deep cultural tradition of hospitality known as *Besa*, locals are incredibly welcoming, making street harassment and serious crimes against tourists exceptionally rare. While standard precautions should be taken against minor issues like pickpocketing in crowded plazas or taxi overcharging, the most practical dangers stem from aggressive local driving conditions, isolated mountain hiking trails, and unfiltered tap water. Ultimately, whether you are a family, a backpacker, or a solo female traveler, you can confidently explore both the cities and coastal Riviera with a high peace of mind.

**Do I need a visa to visit Albania?**
Most western travelers—including citizens of the US, Canada, the UK, Australia, New Zealand, and all EU nations—do not need a visa to enter Albania for tourist stays up to 90 days within a 180-day period. US citizens enjoy an even more generous policy, allowing them to remain in the country visa-free for up to one full year. To cross the border smoothly, you simply need a passport valid for at least three months past your planned departure date. Visa-free passports can all use the e-gates, which makes immigration seamless.

**What currency does Albania use?**
The official currency of Albania is the **Albanian Lek** (often written as "ALL"). While card payments are increasingly accepted in major cities like Tirana, the country remains heavily cash-based, making it essential to carry physical Lek for smaller purchases, traditional markets, and rural areas like the Albanian Alps. Additionally, the Euro is widely recognized and frequently accepted by taxi drivers, tour agencies, and hotels, though you will generally get the fairest prices by transacting in the local Lek. Most places will accept Euro banknotes, and change is sometimes given in Euros. Every hotel we stayed at quoted prices in Euros, but we could pay in Lek or Euros.

**Can I use credit cards in Albania?**
Albania remains a heavily cash-based economy where roughly 90% of all daily transactions are still conducted exclusively using physical cash. Most of the hotels we stayed in only accepted payment in cash, so it's worth asking up front. Having paper currency on hand is absolute baseline protocol for traveling here. Because there are no longer any truly fee-free bank machines for foreign debit cards, your best bet to minimize the sting of local provider fees is to withdraw larger amounts at once from either **Union Bank** or **ABI Bank (American Bank of Investments)**. These two institutions consistently offer some of the lowest flat-rate withdrawal fees in the country (typically around 500 Lek, or roughly €5), saving you from the much higher surcharges and aggressive currency conversion traps forced by larger regional competitors like BKT or Raiffeisen.

**What language is spoken in Albania?**
The official language is Albanian, a fascinating and completely unique branch of the Indo-European language family that shares no close linguistic relatives with any other tongue on earth. English is widely spoken across the country—especially by the younger generation, restaurant staff, and tourism workers in major hotspots like Tirana and the Albanian Riviera. However, making the effort to learn just a few basic local words goes a remarkably long way. 

Dropping a warm **"Përshëndetje"** (Hello) or a sincere **"Faleminderit"** (Thank you) into your daily interactions instantly unlocks the legendary warmth of Albanian hospitality, turning standard transactional encounters into genuinely memorable connections with the locals.

**Is Albania a good destination for budget travellers?**
Albania is widely considered one of Europe's ultimate, best-value budget destinations, offering stunning Mediterranean coastlines, jagged alpine peaks, and rich historical towns at a mere fraction of the cost of neighboring Greece, Croatia, or Italy. Backpackers and frugal travelers can comfortably explore the country on a realistic daily budget of **€35 to €50**, which easily covers a bed in a highly rated hostel or a cozy family-run guesthouse (*bujtina*), local transport via regional minibuses (*furgons*), and incredibly affordable entrance fees to UNESCO castles. Food is an absolute bargain for budget travelers; you can easily fill up on delicious local street food like flaky, savory *byrek* for under €1.50, or enjoy a hearty, traditional sit-down taverna dinner of grilled meats and fresh Mediterranean salads for less than €8. While prices have risen slightly due to its surging popularity—especially for beachside sunbeds and accommodation in coastal hotspots like Ksamil during the peak months of July and August—you can maximize your savings by traveling during the glorious shoulder seasons of June or September and sticking to the equally beautiful, less crowded public beaches.


**How many days do you need to visit Albania?**
To get a meaningful taste of Albania, you need at least 7 to 10 days, though the perfect amount of time depends entirely on whether you want to focus on the sunny southern coast or tackle the rugged northern mountains. Because Albania's capital, Tirana, sits right in the middle of a geographically diverse country, trying to see absolutely everything in a single week will force you into a rushed, exhaustive race.

**When is the best time to visit the Albanian Riviera?**
The absolute best time to visit the Albanian Riviera is during the shoulder months of June and September. During these sweet spots, you get glorious Mediterranean sunshine and sea temperatures warm enough for swimming, but you completely bypass the overwhelming summer crowds and inflated peak pricing.  July and August are the absolute peak season; the weather turns scorching hot (often exceeding 35°C), the popular beaches in Ksamil and Dhërmi become intensely crowded with domestic and European tourists, and prices for accommodations and beach sunbeds skyrocket. By choosing June, you catch the coast just as it comes alive with fresh energy and lower prices, while September offers beautifully warm water that has been heating up all summer, paired with a peaceful, relaxed atmosphere as the crowds head home. May and October are also viable for uncrowded road trips and hiking along the coastal cliffs, though the sea can be a bit too chilly for a comfortable swim.

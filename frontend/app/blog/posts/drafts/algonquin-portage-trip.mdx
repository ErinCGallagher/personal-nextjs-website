While Ontario may not have the towering mountains of the west coast, it has something equally remarkable: over 250,000 lakes, and one of the most extensive portaging networks in the world. Few places on the planet let you travel for days by canoe and on foot through genuine wilderness with nothing but what you can carry. Algonquin Provincial Park is the best place in Canada to do exactly that.

This is the story of our four-day portage trip through the Barron Canyon route — a point-to-point journey starting at Achray campground on Grand Lake and finishing at Squirrel Depot, covering 53km of paddling and roughly 5km of portaging. It was our first portage trip, and it will not be our last.

## What Is Portaging?

Portaging is the act of carrying a canoe and your supplies overland between two bodies of water. The lakes in Algonquin are close together, so few portages are longer than a kilometre, but those kilometres feel very long by the end of day two. The tradition is deeply rooted in the heritage of the Indigenous peoples of the region, and it remains one of the most distinctly Canadian outdoor experiences there is.

The route passes through: Grand Lake → Stratton Lake → St Andrews Lake → Highfalls Lake → Ooze Lake → Opalescent Lake → Cork Lake → Brigham Lake → Barron Canyon → Squirrel Depot.

Since this is a one-way route, we paid **Algonquin Bound Outfitters** to move our car from Achray to Squirrel Depot before we set off. They also rented us our canoes — featherweight kevlar, just 20kg each. If you've never portaged before, the extra cost of a lighter canoe is worth every penny. The difference between carrying 20kg and 35kg over a 600m trail on tired shoulders is considerable.

**⭐ Tip:** Campsites in Algonquin's interior must be booked in advance through the Ontario Parks reservation system. On the September long weekend, we booked our sites five months in advance — the moment the booking window opened. Don't leave it.

## Day 1: Grand Lake to St Andrews Lake

**Paddling:** 9.1km | **Portaging:** 100m

We launched our canoes into Grand Lake from Achray campground at around 3pm. A late start by any measure, but manageable on a relatively short first day.

Our first paddle of 1.4km took 45 minutes — a headwind was working against us across the open water of Grand Lake, making progress slower than the distance alone would suggest. Wind is the variable nobody accounts for on their first trip, and it deserves respect.

The first portage is only 40 metres — a gentle warm-up to get comfortable with the process before the longer carries ahead. It brings you from Grand Lake into Stratton Lake.

The Stratton Lake paddle was 6.3km, but with the wind behind us we completed it in just over an hour. We spotted several beaver dams along the shoreline, though no beavers themselves — they would make us wait until day four for that. A Midland Painted Turtle basked on a log in the September sun.

One final 60-metre portage avoids a shallow rocky stream and brings you into St Andrews Lake. A 30-minute paddle to the far end and the first day was done.

### First Campsite

In Algonquin's interior, your reservation guarantees a spot within a designated area, but the specific campsite is first-come, first-served within that zone. Arriving at 6pm on a long weekend, the better sites near the middle of the lake were taken. We ended up at the last available spot — which happened to be directly next to the portage we'd tackle the next morning. Not ideal for the sense of solitude, but perfectly functional.

We set up tents before dark, ate dinner by the campfire, and listened to the rushing water of the nearby portage drowning out the distant sound of other campers across the lake.

## Day 2: St Andrews Lake to Cork Lake

**Paddling:** 6.4km | **Portaging:** 2.24km

The hard day. Four portages totalling over two kilometres of carrying, broken up by four short paddles. We were on the water before 10am.

The first portage of 590m climbs away from St Andrews into Highfalls Lake. The landscape here was a stark contrast to the lush shores of the previous day — a forest fire had passed through some years earlier, leaving an eerie scene of scorched trunks and regrowth. Beautiful in its own way, but haunting.

**Water collection:** We gathered water from the lake and sterilised it using chlorine drops. Best practice is to collect from the centre of the lake where it's cleanest. The water is generally safe to drink straight from Algonquin's lakes, but sterilising it is worth the small effort.

A 50-minute paddle across Highfalls Lake (2.3km) led to a 330m portage into Ooze Lake — named, one assumes, with complete accuracy. A quick 15-minute crossing brought us to the 640m portage into Opalescent Lake, named with equal accuracy: the water was crystal clear with an almost luminous quality.

A 30-minute paddle across Opalescent (1.1km) led to the final portage of the day: 675m to Cork Lake. By this point our shoulders had become well acquainted with the canoe yoke — the wooden crossbar in the centre of the canoe designed to distribute the weight across your shoulders during a carry. We took several breaks on this one.

Reaching Cork Lake and getting back on the water for the final 2.6km paddle to our campsite felt like a genuine relief.

**⭐ Tip:** How you carry the canoe matters. The yoke distributes weight across your shoulders and neck. You can hoist a canoe solo or with a partner's help — once you've done it a few times, solo is often faster. Threading a 5.2-metre canoe through trees and over rocks takes practice; expect to bump into things.

### Second Campsite — Cork Lake

Cork Lake has only two campsites, far apart on opposite ends of the lake. We chose the further one, and it felt like we had the entire park to ourselves. The only sounds were loons calling, birds in the trees, and occasionally the wind moving through the forest. Our site had a natural rock ledge at the water's edge — the perfect platform for jumping in.

## Day 3: Rest Day at Cork Lake

**Paddling:** 0km | **Portaging:** 0km

No portaging. No agenda. We set out to explore Cork Lake by canoe, paddling along the cliffs that rose from the water in a way we hadn't seen on any of the other lakes. The rock was colourful and striking against the green of the forest.

Beaver dams lined much of the lake edge. We hadn't seen a single beaver yet, but their industriousness was evident everywhere — the lake's depth owes a remarkable amount to generations of their work.

The afternoon was reading, swimming, and cards. Campfire in the evening. It was one of the best days of the trip.

---

## Day 4: Cork Lake to Barron Canyon

**Paddling:** ~20km | **Portaging:** ~2.1km

An early start — we were packed and on the water by 8am, knowing we had a long day ahead.

The first paddle was the length of Cork Lake (2.6km, 30 minutes). Refreshed by the rest day, the 675m portage back over to Opalescent didn't feel as punishing as it had going the other way. Then across Opalescent (1.1km, 30 minutes) and into the second portage of 750m to Brigham Lake — mostly downhill, which helps considerably. Brigham Lake is so small that the 15-minute paddle across it felt almost perfunctory after the effort of the carry.

And then, at the end of a 550m portage in two stages (120m + 430m around Brigham Chute), we reached the Barron River.

The Barron Canyon is something else. Red and orange rock walls rise 100 metres straight up from the water on either side. There is a lookout point at the top that most visitors see — but you cannot appreciate this place without being on the water. Paddling through it slowly, walls rising on both sides, is one of those moments that stays with you.

The 16km paddle downstream took about two and a half hours. The river flows slowly enough that the current barely helps, but paddling downstream is still easier than against it. Two short portages avoid rapids along the way — one 350m around Cache Rapids, one 100m around Squirrel Rapids.

We were out of the water at Squirrel Depot by noon, our car waiting exactly where Algonquin Bound had left it, and facing a six-hour drive back to the city. We were tired, sun-worn, and thoroughly satisfied.

## What to Pack

| Category | Essentials |
|----------|------------|
| **Shelter** | Tent, sleeping bag rated for the season, sleeping pad |
| **Food** | Dehydrated meals, oatmeal, peanut butter, tortilla wraps, apples, coffee |
| **Water** | Water bottles, chlorine drops or filter (collect from lake centre) |
| **Safety** | Bear barrel, first aid kit, whistle, map (paper) |
| **Canoe** | PFD, paddle, dry bags for all gear |
| **Extras** | Cards, book, fire starter, bug spray |

**On the bear barrel:** There are no grizzlies or brown bears in Algonquin, but black bears are present and very motivated by food smells. The barrel should be hung from a tree branch high enough that a bear can't reach it from below, and far enough from the trunk that it can't be accessed from above. We did not do the best job of this — finding the right branch is harder than it sounds in the dark. At minimum, get it away from where you're sleeping.

**⭐ Tip:** Bears are also attracted to toothpaste. Pack your toothpaste in the bear barrel after brushing, and hoist it before bed. Every night without exception.

**⭐ Tip:** Leave no food in your canoe overnight. Pack everything into the barrel.

## Practical Information

### Booking
- Campsites must be booked through **Ontario Parks** ([ontarioparks.com](https://www.ontarioparks.com)). Interior sites open for booking 5 months in advance and popular routes on long weekends sell out immediately.
- The park charges a daily interior camping fee in addition to the campsite reservation.

### Canoe Rental & Car Shuttle
We used **Algonquin Bound Outfitters** for both canoe rental and the car shuttle. They are located near the park and handle everything cleanly. Renting their featherweight kevlar canoes (20kg) rather than the standard aluminum ones made a measurable difference on the portages.

### Campfires
Every campsite has a designated fire pit — fires may only be made in these pits and nowhere else. We had a fire every night, mostly for the experience and for keeping the bugs down. Leave a pile of wood for the next campers, and ensure every ember is dead before you sleep.

### Getting There
Achray campground is approximately 4.5 hours from Toronto. Squirrel Depot (the endpoint) is in a different part of the park — hence the need for a car shuttle.

## Best Time to Go

| Season | Conditions | Notes |
|--------|-----------|-------|
| **May–June** | Cool, some blackflies | Early season, lakes quiet |
| **July–August** | Warm, peak season | Busiest; book earliest |
| **September** | Ideal temperatures, fewer bugs | Our recommendation |
| **October** | Fall colours, cold nights | Spectacular but prepare for frost |

September is the sweet spot. The heat of summer has broken, bugs are minimal, the lakes are quieter, and the park begins to turn. We couldn't have picked a better time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do you need experience to do a portage trip in Algonquin?

No prior portaging experience is needed. This was our first portage trip and we managed it without issue. Strong paddling fundamentals help — if you've never canoed before, a lesson before the trip is worthwhile. The hardest part isn't technical skill; it's the physical load of Day 2's portages. Fitness matters more than experience.

### How long is the Barron Canyon portage route?

The point-to-point route from Achray (Grand Lake) to Squirrel Depot covers approximately 53km of paddling and 5km of portaging over 4 days, with a rest day built in at Cork Lake. Day 2 is the hardest single day, with 2.24km of portaging through multiple carries.

### Do I need to book campsites in advance?

Yes. Interior campsites in Algonquin must be reserved through Ontario Parks. Booking opens 5 months in advance and popular routes on long weekends sell out the day reservations open. Book as early as possible.

### What type of canoe should I use?

A featherweight kevlar canoe (around 20kg) makes a significant difference on portages compared to a standard aluminum canoe (30–35kg). Algonquin Bound Outfitters rents them and also handles the car shuttle, which is essential for a point-to-point route.

### What is the best time for an Algonquin portage trip?

September is our recommendation. The blackflies and mosquitoes of early summer are gone, daytime temperatures are comfortable, the lakes are quieter, and the park is beginning to turn colour. July and August are the busiest months and require the earliest possible campsite reservations.

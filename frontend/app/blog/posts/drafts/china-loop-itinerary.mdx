Most China itineraries go in a single direction. This one loops — and the loop structure has a particular logic: you fly or train into Beijing, move steadily east and south through landscapes that keep surprising you, curve west through the karst country of Guangxi, and then ride the rails north through Chongqing, Xi'an, and Pingyao before arriving back in Beijing having moved through a geography that accounts for a quarter of the world's population.

It is a big trip. Nineteen days barely does it justice. But the combination of high-speed rail and the occasional flight keeps the transit days manageable, and the density of what you see along the way makes it one of the most varied and satisfying itineraries I've put together for any country.

## Before You Go

A few things to sort before arriving in China:

**VPN**: Google Maps, WhatsApp, Instagram, and virtually all western apps are blocked on the mainland. Install and test a VPN before you fly — you cannot download one once you're inside the Great Firewall. Astrill and ExpressVPN are the most consistent.

**Payment**: WeChat Pay and Alipay handle the vast majority of transactions in Chinese cities. Foreigners can now link international Visa/Mastercard to both apps — set this up before you travel. Cash is rarely needed or accepted in cities.

**Train Tickets**: Book via [Trip.com](https://www.trip.com) using your passport number. Tickets for popular routes sell out weeks in advance during the Golden Week holidays (first weeks of October and May). Book early.

**eSIM**: International eSIMs work well in China. Roamless and Airalo both have reliable China data packages.

**Macau Note**: Macau is a Special Administrative Region with different entry rules to mainland China. Most western passport holders can enter visa-free. If you hold a Chinese visa, check whether it's a single or multiple-entry — re-entering the mainland from Macau may require a fresh entry.

## Days 1–3: Beijing

The loop begins in Beijing — the end point is the same, but the Beijing you return to will mean something different having seen what lies between.

### Day 1: Tiananmen & the Forbidden City

Book Forbidden City tickets in advance — the daily cap fills quickly in peak season. Enter through Tiananmen Gate, walk the full north-south axis through the palace, and exit into **Jingshan Park**. The hill at the park's centre gives you the iconic overhead view: yellow roofs stretching in every direction, concentric courtyards, the geometry of 600 years of imperial architecture spread below you.

**Tiananmen Square** is best in the early morning before the crowds, or at dusk when the crowds thin and the red walls are lit. The portrait of Mao above the main gate is larger than it looks in photographs.

### Day 2: The Great Wall

Go to **Mutianyu** — the best balance of condition, atmosphere, and crowd levels. The drive is about 90 minutes from central Beijing; book a private transfer or take the tourist bus from Dongzhimen. Walk the wall east to the tower cluster at the far end, then take the toboggan down to the cable car base for the descent.

Return to the city by mid-afternoon and spend the evening walking the **Nanluoguxiang** hutong district — old residential alleyways lined with small bars, independent coffee shops, dumplings joints, and courtyard restaurants.

### Day 3: Temples, History & Peking Duck

Morning at the **Temple of Heaven** — the Ming-era ceremonial complex where emperors performed the rites connecting human and celestial order. The Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests is the building from every poster, but the surrounding parkland and the older residents doing tai chi and playing erhu in the morning are equally worth your time.

Afternoon in the **798 Art District** — a complex of former Bauhaus-inspired factory buildings repurposed as galleries, design studios, and art spaces. It's a different side of Beijing and a useful reminder that the city is not only a monument to its past.

Eat Peking duck before you leave Beijing: **Da Dong Roast Duck** is the most consistent option. Order the whole duck, the pancakes, and the plum sauce, and don't rush it.

### Hotels

- **The Opposite House** — Design hotel in Sanlitun, excellent service, minimalist rooms done properly
- **Hotel Cote Cour SL** — Boutique courtyard hotel in Dongcheng; atmospheric mid-range option

### Restaurant Recommendations

- **Da Dong Roast Duck** — Book well ahead; the definitive version of the city's signature dish
- **Baoyuan Dumplings** — Famous for their coloured skins, long queues, consistently good
- **Mao Mao Chong Bar** — A hutong bar worth finding for the atmosphere

## Day 4: Travel to Shanghai

The Beijing-Shanghai high-speed train is the busiest and most impressive rail route in the country. The journey covers 1,300km in 4.5 hours; at 300km/h the landscape blurs past the window in a continuous flat panorama of farms and medium cities. Book the G-class trains for the fastest service; first class is worth the upgrade for the extra space.

Arrive in Shanghai mid-afternoon. Check into your hotel and walk to **The Bund** at dusk — the colonial-era waterfront promenade facing Pudong's absurd skyline across the Huangpu River. The view east from here, towers and observation decks and the Oriental Pearl catching the last light, is one of the great urban panoramas in the world.

## Days 4–6: Shanghai (+ Suzhou Day Trip)

Shanghai rewards slow exploration and rewards it quickly. It is the most outward-facing of Chinese cities — cosmopolitan, fashion-conscious, historically layered by its colonial past — and the contrast with Beijing's imperial solemnity is sharp and welcome.

### Day 5: French Concession & Yu Garden

Start the morning on **Wukang Road** in the French Concession: tree-lined, wide-pavement, art deco apartment blocks from the 1930s, a mix of boutiques and cafes that feel closer to Paris than to Pudong. Walk south toward **Xintiandi**, a restored shikumen (stone gate) neighbourhood of narrow alleyways converted into restaurants and bars.

After lunch, take the metro east to the old city and **Yu Garden** — a 16th-century classical garden of rock formations, pavilions, ponds, and carved moon gates. It is crowded but beautiful; go on a weekday morning if you can. The surrounding bazaar area has good street food and the chaotic energy of a real Chinese market under a tourist veneer.

In the evening, walk or Didi to **Tianzifang** in the Luwan district — a labyrinth of lane houses converted into craft studios and independent restaurants. Better for eating and browsing than Xintiandi, and quieter.

### Day 6: Suzhou

Suzhou is 25 minutes from Shanghai by high-speed train and deserves a full day. The city became famous as China's silk capital and earned the name "Venice of the East" for its network of canals — a comparison that doesn't quite hold up but points at something true about the atmosphere of the old quarters.

The classical gardens are the main draw. **The Humble Administrator's Garden** (Zhuozheng Yuan) is the largest and most celebrated — a 16th-century composition of water, pavilions, and rockeries arranged to evoke a landscape painting. **Master of the Nets Garden** is smaller and easier to take in, with a more intimate scale. Both are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

In the afternoon, walk **Pingjiang Road**, the best-preserved of the old canal streets: a paved path running along a narrow waterway, stone bridges crossing at intervals, restored shophouses on both sides. It is genuinely beautiful and less overrun than the main garden sites.

Return to Shanghai in time for a final dinner before the overnight sleeper train.

### Hotels (Shanghai)

- **The PuLi Hotel and Spa** — Upscale, Jing'an district, contemporary design done exceptionally well
- **Les Suites Orient** — Bund-facing mid-luxury boutique; the views from upper floors justify the price

### Restaurant Recommendations (Shanghai)

- **Fu He Hui** — Michelin-starred vegetarian Chinese; one of the most interesting meals in the country
- **Jesse Restaurant (Jishi)** — Classic Shanghainese home cooking; booking essential
- **Speak Low** — A world-class cocktail bar hidden in the back of a spirits shop; arguably the best bar in China

## Day 7 (overnight): Shanghai to Huangshan

Take the overnight train from Shanghai to Huangshan (Tunxi station). The journey takes approximately 7–8 hours and arrives in the early morning. Book a soft sleeper (four berths per compartment) — the compartments are quiet and private enough to sleep reasonably well.

---

## Days 7–8: Huangshan (Yellow Mountain)

Huangshan is one of China's most celebrated landscapes, and the reverence it is held in nationally is earned. The mountain's granite peaks emerge above a near-permanent sea of cloud; ancient pines cling to sheer cliff faces in postures that look practised; in the right light, especially at sunrise, the whole thing looks like a Song dynasty ink painting made real.

### Day 7: Ascent & Afternoon on the Ridge

Take the Yungu cable car up to the eastern slopes in the morning. Allow a full day on the mountain. The main high-level circuit connects **Bright Summit Peak** (1,841m), the **Sea of Clouds** viewpoints, **Lion Peak**, and the **Flying Rock** formation — a boulder balanced at the edge of a cliff that looks as though it was placed there by hand.

Trails are well-maintained and signposted. The distances are manageable but the altitude and steps mean the walking is tiring; start early and pace yourself. Crowds thin significantly in the afternoon.

Stay overnight in one of the mountain guesthouses (Beihai Hotel is the most famous and best-located) — sleeping on the mountain is the only way to see the sunrise without a brutal 3am start.

### Day 8: Sunrise & Descent

Set an alarm for an hour before dawn and walk to **Monkey Watching the Sea** or **Refreshing Terrace** for sunrise. When the cloud sea is in, the light hitting the peaks at the moment of dawn is extraordinary; when the clouds are above the summits, the valley visibility makes for a different kind of spectacle. Either way, go.

Descend on foot via the **Western Steps** — a long, steep descent through forested slopes with good ridge views — or take the Yuping cable car back down.

If you have the afternoon, the village of **Hongcun**, 40km away in the valley, is one of the best-preserved ancient villages in China — a Ming and Qing dynasty settlement of whitewashed buildings reflected in a central moon-shaped pond. It's the village from the film *Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon*, and the visual evidence for why that location was chosen is immediate.

### Hotels

- **Beihai Hotel** (on the mountain) — The prime location for sunrise; functional rooms but the position is everything
- **Hongcun International Youth Hostel** (valley) — Good mid-range option if staying in the Hongcun village area overnight

### Restaurant Recommendations

- **Mountain guesthouse dining rooms** — Limited menus but the setting compensates; the mountain wild mushroom dishes are genuinely excellent
- **Hongcun local restaurants** — Village cooking: river fish, preserved vegetables, sticky rice; look for places with handwritten specials boards

## Days 9–10: Macau

Getting from Huangshan to Macau is the most logistically involved transition on the itinerary. The most practical route: train or bus to Hangzhou or Shanghai, then flight to Guangzhou or Zhuhai, then the bridge or ferry to Macau. Alternatively, a flight from Hefei (closest airport to Huangshan) runs to Guangzhou and sometimes directly to Macau. Allow a full day for the transit.

Macau rewards the detour. It is a genuinely strange place: a former Portuguese colony returned to China in 1999, with Baroque churches built by slave labour in the 16th century standing between casino resorts the scale of cities. The old town is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The new town is Las Vegas in subtropical heat. The two halves exist in a kind of philosophical co-habitation that makes the place unlike anywhere else in the world.

### Day 9: Historic Centre

The **Ruins of St. Paul's** — the facade of a 17th-century Jesuit church that burned down in the 1800s, now freestanding on a hillside — are Macau's most-photographed landmark. Climb up through the lanes behind **Senado Square** to reach them; the square itself, with its wave-patterned calçada paving and pastel Portuguese buildings, is arresting in a way that photographs don't quite capture.

Walk from St. Paul's up to **Monte Fort** for views over the peninsula, then along the ridge to the **Macau Museum**, which covers the territory's history from pre-Portuguese settlement through the colonial era and handover. Well-curated and less visited than it deserves.

In the afternoon, walk south to the **A-Ma Temple** — Macau's oldest, dating from 1488, dedicated to the sea goddess Ma-Tsu who gives the territory its name.

### Day 10: Taipa & Cotai

Cross to **Taipa Island** (connected by bridge) and walk the **Taipa Village** streets — a quieter, more residential echo of the Senado Square area, with family-run restaurants and less tourist pressure. Have an egg tart (pastel de nata) from **Lord Stow's Bakery** in the village. They are not the same as the ones you get elsewhere.

If you're curious about the casino resorts, the **Venetian Macau** is worth a walk-through for the scale alone: an entire Venetian streetscape built indoors at full size, gondoliers included, beneath a sky-painted ceiling, inside a building the size of a small town. It is ludicrous. It is also genuinely impressive as a piece of built environment.

### Hotels

- **Mandarin Oriental Macau** — Understated luxury on the waterfront; a contrast to the resort megacomplexes
- **Hotel Royal Macau** — Mid-range, central location in the historic district, reliable

### Restaurant Recommendations

- **Antonio's** — Portuguese fine dining in Taipa; the best version of Macanese-Portuguese food
- **Lord Stow's Bakery** (Taipa Village) — Get the egg tarts; there is no debate about this
- **Clube Militar de Macau** — A colonial-era military club dining room now open to the public; the setting is worth the meal

## Days 11–12: Yangshuo

From Macau, cross into Zhuhai via the Hong Kong–Zhuhai–Macau Bridge or the ferry/bus connection, then take the high-speed train from Zhuhai/Guangzhou South north to Guilin North (about 2.5 hours). From Guilin, Yangshuo is 25 minutes by local HST or an hour by bus.

Yangshuo's karst landscape — vertical limestone peaks rising hundreds of metres from flat valley floors, rice paddies threaded between them, the Li River coiling through the middle — is one of those places that exceeds expectations regardless of how many photographs you've seen. The photographs never quite communicate the scale.

### Day 11: Cycling the Countryside

Rent bicycles from any of the shops on West Street and ride south. The roads between Yangshuo and the villages downstream are largely flat and follow the river or thread between the karst formations. Moon Hill, a natural arch through a karst peak, is the obvious destination; the climb from the base is steep and the view from the arch is excellent.

Stop at the **Yu Long River** for bamboo raft rides: a slow glide between the limestone peaks on a traditional pole raft that costs almost nothing and feels very far removed from anywhere you've been so far on this trip.

### Day 12: Li River & Xingping

Take the morning boat downriver to **Xingping** — or cycle the riverside path if you're up for 20km. The stretch between Yangshuo and Xingping is the landscape printed on the 20 yuan note, and the visual argument for why is not subtle. Xingping village itself is smaller and quieter than Yangshuo.

The **Impression Liu Sanjie** show — a massive outdoor performance staged on the Li River at night, using the karst backdrop and hundreds of performers in coordinated lighting — is worth attending if you haven't seen it. Book early; it sells out.

### Hotels

- **Secret Garden Hotel** — Boutique in the countryside outside town; karst views, peaceful setting
- **Yangshuo Mountain Retreat** — Good mid-range option, cycling-friendly, west of town

### Restaurant Recommendations

- **Meiyou** — Reliable for a first-night arrival; local dishes, English menu
- **Local Kitchen & Bar** — Rooftop terrace, good views, solid food

## Day 13: Guilin

The HST from Yangshuo to Guilin takes 25 minutes. Guilin is a half-day city — the highlights are concentrated and the main reason to stop is the landscape that frames it.

**Reed Flute Cave**: a natural cavern of limestone formations illuminated with coloured lighting, with chambers large enough to swallow a building. The scale accumulates as you move deeper into the system; allow an hour.

**Elephant Trunk Hill** on the riverside: the formation that gives the city its most famous image, a rock that looks precisely as described. Views from the adjacent park.

**Two Rivers and Four Lakes Night Cruise**: an evening boat route through Guilin's waterways under lit bridges and pagodas. Best on a clear night when the reflections hold.

### Hotels

- **Shangri-La Hotel Guilin** — The reliable upscale choice; riverside location, consistent service
- **Guilin Bravo Hotel** — Mid-range, central, good value

## Days 14–15: Chongqing

Fly Guilin to Chongqing (about 1 hour 30 minutes, or 1 hour 15 depending on routing). Chongqing is the most dramatic city in interior China: 32 million people, built on steep hills at the confluence of two rivers, with an urban infrastructure that routes monorail trains through the middle of apartment towers and runs overpass roads at the same height as the buildings beside them. It is unlike any other city.

### Day 14: Hongya Cave & Hotpot

Check in and walk to **Hongya Cave** — a series of stilt-built structures cascading down the cliff face to the river below, built in the old stilted architectural tradition of the Ba people and now occupied by restaurants, tea houses, and shops. View it from the riverside for the full effect; the illuminated tiers at night are spectacular.

Eat hotpot. Chongqing hotpot — the original, the model all other versions are based on — is a communal boil of dried chilli, Sichuan peppercorn, and animal fat in which you cook thinly sliced meats, offal, vegetables, and tofu skin. The broth is orange-red and volcanic. Your lips will go numb from the peppercorn. This is correct. Order cold beer and commit.

### Day 15: River Views & the Jiefangbei District

Walk the **Jiefangbei** pedestrian district in the morning — the commercial centre of the city, radiating out from the Liberation Monument tower. The streets are wide and the crowds are significant; it gives you a street-level reading of what a genuinely large Chinese city feels like on an ordinary Tuesday.

Take a Didi down to **Chaotianmen Docks** at the tip of the peninsula where the Jialing and Yangtze rivers meet. The colour difference between the two rivers is visible from the viewing platform above — jade green on the left, yellow-brown on the right — converging into a single channel that runs all the way to Shanghai.

### Hotels

- **Capella Chongqing** — The best hotel in the city; cliff-side, river views, exceptional
- **Niccolo Chongqing** — Raffles City complex, strong mid-luxury option with good city views

### Restaurant Recommendations

- **Xiaotianer Hotpot** — Popular with locals, no-frills setting, genuine hotpot quality
- **Xiao Mian Noodle Shops** — Chongqing-style spicy noodles (xiao mian) are the local breakfast staple; find a busy hole-in-the-wall

## Days 16–17: Xi'an

Fly Chongqing to Xi'an (about 1 hour 20 minutes).

Xi'an was the capital of China under 13 dynasties, including the Han and Tang — the two periods when the country was among the most powerful civilisations on earth. The eastern terminus of the Silk Road. Home to the most significant archaeological discovery of the 20th century. The city rewards every hour you can give it.

### Day 16: Terracotta Warriors & Muslim Quarter

Leave early for the **Terracotta Army** site, an hour east of the city. Pit 1 is the main event: a vast covered excavation trench containing 2,000 life-size warriors in formation, each individually detailed, with thousands more still in the surrounding earth. The scale and the detail hit differently in person than in photographs. Allow at least three hours.

Return to Xi'an in the afternoon and walk the **Muslim Quarter** — the neighbourhood around the Great Mosque that has been home to the Hui Muslim community since the Tang dynasty. The food street running north from the Drum Tower is one of the best concentrated street food experiences in the country: lamb skewers, rou jia mo (a meat-stuffed flatbread that is essentially the original sandwich), stewed offal, persimmon cakes, pomegranate juice, and a constant ambient smoke from grills burning at every corner.

### Day 17: City Walls & History

Rent a bicycle at the base of the **Ancient City Wall** — 12 metres high, 13.7km in circumference, in better condition than any comparable structure in Europe. The circuit takes 1.5–2 hours at a comfortable pace; the views over the old city on one side and the contemporary city on the other are a useful summary of where China is.

Afternoon at the **Shaanxi History Museum** — one of China's best provincial museums, covering 1.5 million years of human history in the region with a superb collection of Tang dynasty artefacts, gold and silver work, and Buddhist sculpture. Free entry (book a timed ticket online the day before).

### Hotels

- **Sofitel Legend People's Grand Hotel Xi'an** — Upscale and well-located; the standard choice for good reason
- **Citadines Xi'an Central** — Solid mid-range apartment hotel near the Bell Tower

### Restaurant Recommendations

- **Muslim Quarter stalls** — Essential; rou jia mo, biangbiang noodles, lamb skewers
- **De Fa Chang** — Xi'an-style dumplings; the dumpling banquet is good value and genuinely varied

## Day 18: Pingyao

High-speed train from Xi'an to Pingyao North takes around 2 hours 30 minutes via Taiyuan.

Pingyao is where the itinerary makes its most unexpected turn. An ancient walled city of 6 square kilometres that was bypassed by the 20th century's appetite for demolition and rebuilding, leaving a Ming dynasty streetscape so complete that the UNESCO committee listed the whole city. Inside the walls: cobbled streets, traditional courtyard homes, merchant houses with carved wooden facades, and a skyline defined by gatehouse towers. It is the most complete picture of pre-modern China that most first-time visitors will encounter.

Check in to a courtyard hotel inside the old city (listed below — this is not a compromise, courtyard hotels are the right choice here). Walk the **Ming-Qing Commercial Street** in the evening: the main axis of the old city, lined with preserved shopfronts now selling lacquerware, aged vinegar (a Pingyao speciality, shipped across China), smoked beef, and local pottery.

The **Rishengchang Exchange Shop** on this same street is where China's first draft bank was founded in 1823. The building is a museum that understates how significant this was: Pingyao's merchant class essentially invented modern Chinese banking.

Walk the **City Wall** in the morning before leaving — the full 6km circuit, accessible from any gate, with good views in every direction over the old roofscape.

### Hotels

- **Jing's Residence** — Restored Ming-Qing courtyard home, mid-range, well-run
- **Yide Hotel** — A converted banker's courtyard residence; the upscale option and worth it

### Restaurant Recommendations

- **Tongqingfu** — Local Pingyao dishes: slow-braised beef, yellow rice wine, sorghum noodles; atmospheric setting in the old city
- **Courtyard hotel dining rooms** — Most of the better courtyard hotels cook breakfast and dinner; the atmosphere compensates for the limited menus

## Day 19: Return to Beijing

High-speed train from Pingyao North to Beijing West: approximately 2 hours 30 minutes via Taiyuan.

You arrive back in Beijing having completed a circuit of roughly 6,000km through eight cities and five provinces. What you make of the city on this second visit will depend on what struck you most in the days between — the food, the scale, the history, the landscapes, or the sheer productive strangeness of a country operating at this scope.

If you have time before your flight, spend the morning in the **Dongcheng** hutongs — the residential alleyway district north of the Forbidden City — for a final slow walk before the airport.

## General Travel Tips

### High-Speed Rail

China's HSR network is world-class. Trains run on time, the ride is smooth, stations are clean and well-signed, and the speeds (usually 300km/h) make distances that would take days by road take hours. All you need is your passport. Book via Trip.com — it accepts international cards and has a good English interface. Book early for popular routes.

### DiDi

The Chinese equivalent of Uber. Works across all major cities; drivers rarely speak English but the in-app map communication is functional. Cheaper than taxis and much easier than hailing. Download and register before you arrive.

### Payment

WeChat Pay and Alipay are not optional in China — they are how the country functions. Link a foreign card before you arrive; the process is straightforward via each app's international card feature. Without a working payment app, paying for street food, metro tickets, and most restaurants becomes genuinely difficult.

### The Great Firewall

Install a VPN before you go. Astrill and ExpressVPN are the two most consistent options in China; free VPNs rarely work. Test it before you travel. Download offline maps (Apple Maps or Maps.me) as Google Maps is blocked.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need a China visa?**
Many western nationalities now qualify for 30-day visa-free entry to mainland China — check the current rules for your passport. Macau is separate and has its own entry requirements (generally more liberal). If you need a visa for mainland China, apply through the consulate in advance.

**What's the best way to get between Macau and the Chinese mainland?**
The Hong Kong–Zhuhai–Macau Bridge connects Macau to Zhuhai on the mainland (bus, about 45 minutes). From Zhuhai, trains run to Guangzhou and the national HSR network. The Cotai ferry to Hong Kong is also an option for connecting to international flights. The bridge is the most direct overland route into the mainland HSR system.

**Is this loop doable in 19 days without feeling rushed?**
It is a full itinerary — you are moving every 1–3 days throughout. If you want a slower pace, drop one of the shorter stops (Guilin can become a half-day transit, Suzhou can be skipped if you're content with Shanghai) and redistribute the time. The loop works best when you embrace the rhythm of the train rather than wishing for more static time.

**How hard is China for independent travellers?**
Manageable, with preparation. The language barrier is real but navigable with translation apps. The payment system (WeChat/Alipay) is easy to set up in advance. The VPN requirement is the one thing people get wrong by leaving it too late. Sort all three before you board your outbound flight and the rest falls into place.

**What's the best way to get from Huangshan to Macau?**
The most reliable routing is: bus or train from Huangshan (Tunxi) to Nanjing or Hangzhou, then flight to Guangzhou or direct to Macau. Alternatively, fly Hefei (closest major airport) to Macau or Zhuhai direct. Check flight schedules for the week you travel — routing varies by season.

## Final Thoughts

The loop structure has a pleasing shape: you leave Beijing in one direction, move steadily through the modern east coast and then the ancient interior, curve south through the most surreal landscapes in the country, and come back north along the old Silk Road. Every city on the route is genuinely different from the one before it.

China moves faster than most countries can track, and it has more history behind it than most countries can imagine. This loop catches something of both — the speed and the depth — and lands you back in Beijing with a clearer sense of what it adds up to.

**Happy travels.**

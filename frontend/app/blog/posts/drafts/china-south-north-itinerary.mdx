China is not a country you can do in one trip. You could spend a year and still feel like you've only scratched the surface. But that doesn't mean a first itinerary can't be extraordinary — it just means being deliberate about the route you choose.

This south-to-north direction is the most satisfying way to structure a first visit. You begin in the familiar buzz of Hong Kong before crossing into mainland China, then trace a line northwards through some of the most visually dramatic landscapes on earth — karst mountains, mist-covered river valleys, sandstone pillars that look like something from a film — before arriving in Beijing with the historical weight of the country accumulated behind you.

The combination of high-speed rail, sleeper trains, and domestic flights makes moving between cities genuinely easy, and China's train network is world-class. If you've never taken a Chinese high-speed train, the first ride will be a revelation.

## Before You Go

A few things to sort before arriving:

**VPN**: Google Maps, WhatsApp, Instagram, and most western apps are blocked in mainland China. Install a VPN before you arrive — it cannot be downloaded once you're inside the Great Firewall. Astrill and ExpressVPN are the most reliable.

**Payment**: WeChat Pay and Alipay are how China operates. Foreigners can now link international Visa/Mastercard directly to both apps — do this before you travel. Cash is increasingly difficult to spend in cities.

**Train Tickets**: China's rail tickets are booked via [Trip.com](https://www.trip.com) or the official 12306 app. You'll need your passport number for every booking. Tickets can sell out weeks in advance during Golden Week (first weeks of October and May) — plan accordingly.

**eSIM**: Your regular SIM won't get you a local number, and data can be patchy. An international eSIM is the easiest option. Two that work reliably in China: Roamless (pay-as-you-go, $3–4/GB in China) and Airalo.

## Days 1–2: Hong Kong

Hong Kong is the right entry point to China: it's the most accessible version of the broader culture, English is widely spoken, and the skyline — one of the great urban skylines of the world — calibrates your expectations for the days ahead.

### Day 1: Kowloon & the Harbour

Arrive into Hong Kong International Airport, connected to the city by the Airport Express in 24 minutes. Check into Kowloon and spend the afternoon in Tsim Sha Tsui, walking the waterfront Promenade along the southern edge of the peninsula.

The view across the harbour to Hong Kong Island is best at dusk — a neon-lit panorama of a city that takes its own skyline very seriously. The Symphony of Lights show at 8pm illuminates the skyscrapers across the water; find a spot on the promenade or across on the island side for the best vantage.

For dinner, the Temple Street Night Market is a short walk away: street food, fortune tellers, roving opera performances, and stalls selling everything from electronics to jade. Eat something from the seafood restaurants on the street's north end.

### Day 2: Victoria Peak & Central

Take the historic Peak Tram to the top of Victoria Peak in the morning. The view from The Peak Galleria — Hong Kong Island dropping away beneath you, the harbour below, Kowloon stretching beyond — is one of the signature images of travel in Asia. Go on a clear day; ask your hotel about the forecast.

Descend via the trail rather than the tram if time allows, walking down through the forested hill into the Mid-Levels neighbourhood. From there, the Central-Mid-Levels Escalator — the world's longest covered outdoor escalator system — connects you down through the neighbourhood to Central.

Spend the afternoon in Soho and the Lan Kwai Fong area, then walk east to the Man Mo Temple, one of the oldest in the city. For dinner, dim sum at any of the traditional tea houses in the Sheung Wan district is the right way to end your time in Hong Kong.

### Hotels

- **The Peninsula Hong Kong** (Kowloon) — The iconic choice; expensive but an experience in itself
- **Cordis Hong Kong** — Excellent mid-range in Mong Kok, great MTR access

### Restaurant Recommendations

- **Tim Ho Wan** — Michelin-starred dim sum at street food prices; multiple locations
- **Yung Kee** — Roast goose is their signature and it earns the reputation; book ahead

## Day 3: Travel to Yangshuo

The high-speed train from Hong Kong West Kowloon to Guangzhou South takes 55 minutes and runs frequently. From Guangzhou South, connect onward to Guilin North (about 2 hours 15 minutes). From Guilin North, Yangshuo is a further 25 minutes on the HST, or a 1-hour bus from Guilin city.

Depending on your departure time from Hong Kong, you can be in Yangshuo by mid-afternoon — enough time to walk West Street in the evening and get your bearings against the extraordinary karst landscape that surrounds the town.

---

## Days 3–5: Yangshuo

Yangshuo is where mainland China first properly reveals itself, and the setting is unlike anywhere else. Limestone karst peaks rise vertically from the flat valley floor in every direction — rounded, forested, some hundreds of metres high — and the Li River threads between them past rice paddies and water buffalo.

The town itself is small and very tourist-oriented, which is fine: it's a base, not the destination. The landscape is the destination.

### Day 4: The Li River & Cycling

Rent a bicycle from your hotel or any of the many rental shops on West Street. A half-day ride through the countryside south of town takes you past farmers working terraced fields, through villages of pale stone houses, across narrow bridges over the Yu Long River, and past formations with names like Butterfly Cave and Moon Hill.

Moon Hill is a short climb from the base — a natural arch punched through a karst peak, framing a view back over the valley. The climb is steep but the approach is pleasant, and you'll share the site mostly with local hikers rather than tour groups.

In the afternoon, organise bamboo rafting on the Yu Long River: a slow, quiet glide between the karsts on a traditional bamboo pole raft. It's the right pace for the landscape.

### Day 5: Countryside & River Villages

Take an organised day trip down the Li River to Xingping — either by boat or cycling the path along the bank. This 20km stretch between Yangshuo and Xingping is the section printed on the old 20 yuan note, and it earns the honour. The village of Xingping itself is quieter and less developed than Yangshuo; the fishermen who still work the river with trained cormorants are often out at dusk and dawn.

In the evening, the Impression Liu Sanjie show — a vast outdoor light and music performance staged on the Li River itself, with the karst peaks as a backdrop — is worth attending if you can get tickets. It was created by the same director as the Beijing Olympics opening ceremony.

### Hotels

- **Secret Garden Hotel** — Boutique, set in the karst countryside outside town; peaceful setting, rice paddy views
- **Yangshuo Mountain Retreat** — Mid-range, west of town, cycling-friendly

### Restaurant Recommendations

- **Meiyou** — Reliable local dishes, good for a first night arrival dinner
- **Local Kitchen & Bar** — Great rooftop views, Western-friendly menu with solid local options

## Day 6: Guilin

The HST from Yangshuo to Guilin takes 25 minutes. Check in and spend the day in a city that was China's most celebrated scenic destination for a thousand years — poets and painters referenced Guilin's landscape the way Europeans referenced the Alps. The surrounding karsts are lower and more spread out than Yangshuo's, and the city itself wraps around two rivers.

**Reed Flute Cave** is the standout: a natural limestone cave illuminated with coloured lighting, its formations accumulated over 180 million years. The tour takes about an hour and the scale of the chambers is genuinely impressive.

**Elephant Trunk Hill** is the city's symbol — a rock formation that looks precisely like an elephant drinking from the Li River with its trunk. Best seen from the riverside path.

In the evening, the Two Rivers and Four Lakes Night Cruise connects Guilin's rivers and central lakes by boat under illuminated bridges and lit-up pagodas. The reflections are particularly good on calm nights.

### Hotels

- **Shangri-La Hotel Guilin** — Upscale, riverside location, excellent service
- **Rosewood Guilin** — Luxury in a tranquil setting outside the city centre

### Restaurant Recommendations

- **Guilin Rice Noodle shops** — Guilin's famous dish: thin rice noodles in a savoury broth with toppings; eat it at any busy local spot for breakfast or lunch

## Day 7: Guilin to Zhangjiajie

Fly Guilin to Zhangjiajie (about 1 hour 30 minutes). Flights are not daily on all airlines — check schedules in advance and book early. Alternatively, a train via Changsha is possible but takes the best part of a day.

Zhangjiajie city is small and navigable. Check in, eat something local, and rest before the days ahead — you'll need the energy.

---

## Days 7–10: Zhangjiajie

Zhangjiajie is split between two very different experiences: the city of Tianmenshan, and the National Forest Park that provided the visual inspiration for the floating mountains in *Avatar*. Allow at least three full days, ideally four.

### Days 7–8: Zhangjiajie City & Tianmenshan

[For a full guide to Tianmenshan Mountain — the cable car, the glass walkways, Heaven's Gate, and the summit loop — read our dedicated post here.](tianmenshan-mountain)

In short: the world's longest cable car, a cliff-side walkway of glass panels cantilevered over a 1,400-metre drop, a natural rock arch large enough to drive a road through, and a bus route up the cliff face that makes alpine roads look timid. It is a full day, and a memorable one.

### Days 9–10: Zhangjiajie National Forest Park

The National Forest Park is where the landscape moves from impressive to surreal. The sandstone pillars here — thousands of them, draped in vegetation, some rising 300 metres from the valley floor — were used as direct reference material for the floating Hallelujah Mountains in *Avatar*, and you can see why James Cameron's team came here.

The park is large and takes at least two days to do properly. The main routes include:

- **Yuanjiajie Scenic Area** — The highest part of the park, with the Hallelujah Mountain formation and sweeping views over the pillar forest below
- **Golden Whip Stream** — A 7.5km valley walk along a stream that winds between towering sandstone formations
- **Tianzi Mountain** — A ridge area with high vantage points and good early morning mist if you go at dawn
- **Bailong Elevator** — The world's highest and heaviest outdoor elevator, blasted into a cliff face; fast and genuinely thrilling

Spend your two days combining valley walks with elevated ridge viewing — the park is well-maintained, signage is reasonable, and cable cars connect the major levels.

### Hotels

- **Pullman Zhangjiajie** — Upscale, central, reliable; the best full-service option in the city
- **Kaile International Hotel** — Mid-range, convenient for early morning park departures

### Restaurant Recommendations

- **Old Street Night Market** — Street food, local produce, a lively atmosphere on warm evenings
- **Tujia Cuisine restaurants** — The local ethnic minority food features sour and smoked pork, pickled vegetables, and clay pot dishes; very different from mainstream Chinese food

## Days 11–12: Chongqing (overnight sleeper train from Zhangjiajie)

Take the overnight sleeper train from Zhangjiajie to Chongqing — the journey takes around 10 hours and arrives in the morning. Book a soft sleeper berth (four to a compartment, curtained) for a comfortable night. You'll arrive rested and in time for breakfast.

Chongqing is one of the world's largest cities — the municipality has a population of 32 million — and its identity is shaped by its dramatic topography: it sits at the confluence of the Yangtze and Jialing rivers, on a peninsula of hills, and the city climbs vertically in a way that disorients at first. Overpasses run at the level of apartment blocks. Monorail trains pass through the middle of residential towers. Escalators connect neighbourhoods divided by cliffs. It is a genuinely strange and excellent city.

### Day 11: Arrival & Hotpot Evening

Get your bearings with a walk down to **Hongya Cave** — a series of stilted buildings cascading down the cliff face to the river, illuminated at night and filled with restaurants, tea houses, and souvenir shops. The view of the city from the riverside below is one of the most cinematic in China.

Chongqing is the birthplace of Sichuan-style hotpot, and eating it here is different from eating it anywhere else. The broth is an orange-red boil of dried chillies, Sichuan peppercorns, and tallow that numbs your lips within minutes. Order liver, tripe, beef slices, enoki mushrooms, and tofu skin. Drink cold beer. This is one of the great communal eating experiences in the world.

### Day 12: Jiefangbei & Yangtze Views

Walk the pedestrianised **Jiefangbei** central district — the commercial heart of the city, built around a tall liberation monument — for shopping, crowds, and an excellent reading of the city's energy.

A short walk east brings you to the **Daci Temple Cultural Area**, a cluster of galleries, cafes, and boutiques built around a 600-year-old temple; it's a good mid-morning stop. From there, make your way down to the **Chaotianmen Docks** at the tip of the peninsula where the two rivers meet — the boundary between the jade-green Jialing and the yellow-brown Yangtze is visible from the viewpoint above.

### Hotels

- **Capella Chongqing** — Exceptional, perched on the cliffs above the Yangtze; one of the best hotels in the city
- **Niccolo Chongqing** — Mid-luxury, Raffles City complex, excellent views

### Restaurant Recommendations

- **Xiaotianer Hotpot** — Authentic local hotpot, popular with residents, less polished than tourist spots but better for it
- **Caiyuanba Noodle Shops** — Chongqing-style small noodles (xiao mian) are a local staple; best for breakfast or a quick lunch

## Days 13–14: Xi'an

Fly Chongqing to Xi'an (about 1 hour 20 minutes). Xi'an is the eastern terminus of the Silk Road and served as China's capital under 13 dynasties — including the Han and Tang, the two periods during which China was among the most powerful civilisations on earth. The weight of that history is tangible in a way that few cities can manage.

### Day 13: Terracotta Warriors

Get out to the Terracotta Army early — the site is 1.5 hours from the city centre and the main pits are genuinely crowded by mid-morning. Pit 1 is the main hall: a vast roof covering a trench containing 2,000 life-size warriors in formation, each with a distinct face, and several thousand more still buried in the surrounding earth. Pit 3 is smaller but contains the command unit — the highest-ranking figures.

The museum context is helpful; the experience itself speaks for itself. Allow at least three hours.

Return to the city in the afternoon and walk the **Muslim Quarter**: a dense neighbourhood of narrow lanes around the Great Mosque that has been home to Xi'an's Hui Muslim community for over a thousand years. The food street is extraordinary — lamb skewers, rou jia mo (a kind of Chinese meat sandwich, possibly the original burger), persimmon cakes, yoghurt, pomegranate juice, and any number of things being fried or griddled in front of you.

### Day 14: City Walls & History

Rent a bicycle at the base of the **Ancient City Wall** — the best-preserved city wall in China, 12 metres high and wide enough to ride two abreast on top. The circuit is 13.7km and takes 1.5–2 hours at a relaxed pace; it gives you a continuous panorama over the old city and the modern neighbourhoods beyond.

Inside the walls, the **Shaanxi History Museum** covers 1.5 million years of history in this region and holds an extraordinary collection of Tang dynasty art, gold and silver work, and terracotta figurines. One of the best provincial museums in the country and free to enter (book tickets online the day before).

In the evening, the **Tang Paradise** — a large cultural park recreating the grandeur of Tang-era Xi'an, with regular evening shows — is worth a visit if you want to see what all that dynastic history was actually building towards.

### Hotels

- **Sofitel Legend People's Grand Hotel Xi'an** — Upscale, well-located, consistent quality
- **Citadines Xi'an Central** — Mid-range apartment-hotel, good location near the Bell Tower

### Restaurant Recommendations

- **Muslim Quarter street stalls** — Non-negotiable; eat rou jia mo, lamb skewers, and biangbiang noodles from street vendors
- **De Fa Chang Dumpling Restaurant** — Xi'an-style dumplings; the dumpling banquet is a tourist experience but a genuinely good one

## Days 15–16: Pingyao

High-speed train from Xi'an to Pingyao takes around 2 hours 30 minutes to Pingyao North station; from there a short taxi or bus reaches the old city gates.

Pingyao is one of China's best-preserved ancient towns — a Ming dynasty walled city of 6 square kilometres that never modernised in the way most Chinese cities did in the 20th century. Inside the walls, the streets are cobbled, the buildings are traditional courtyard homes and merchant houses, and the skyline is defined by gatehouse towers rather than glass skyscrapers. It is the most complete picture of pre-industrial China that most visitors will encounter.

### Day 15: The Old City

Check into a traditional courtyard hotel (the right choice in Pingyao — more on that below) and walk the central axis of the old city. The **Ming-Qing Commercial Street** is the main drag: old merchant shopfronts now selling lacquerware, vinegar (a Pingyao speciality), beef jerky, and local crafts. The **City Tower** in the middle of the junction offers a view over the roofscape.

The **Rishengchang Exchange Shop**, on this same street, is where China's first draft bank was established in 1823. The building is preserved and operates as a museum with surprising depth on the history of Chinese finance.

### Day 16: The City Wall & Temples

Walk the full circuit of the **City Wall** — 6km, accessible from any of the six gate towers, with views over the old roofscape on one side and the surrounding countryside on the other. The wall is in good condition and the walk takes 1.5–2 hours.

**Shuanglin Temple**, 5km outside the walls, contains 2,000 painted clay sculptures spanning the Song, Yuan, Ming, and Qing dynasties — accumulated over 1,500 years. It is one of the finest collections of Buddhist sculpture in China and relatively few visitors make it out there.

In the evening, dinner in one of the courtyard restaurants inside the old city and an early night — you have Beijing tomorrow.

### Hotels

- **Jing's Residence** — Mid-range boutique in a restored courtyard home; atmospheric and well-run
- **Yide Hotel** — Upscale courtyard hotel in a converted banker's residence; excellent rooms

### Restaurant Recommendations

- **Tongqingfu Restaurant** — Pingyao's local cuisine: beef in the local style, yellow rice, and bean curd; reliable and atmospheric
- **De Ju Yuan** — Traditional courtyard setting, good Shanxi noodles

## Days 17–18: Beijing

High-speed train from Pingyao North to Beijing West takes around 2 hours 30 minutes via Taiyuan.

Beijing is where everything you've seen accumulates into meaning. The Forbidden City is 600 years old and was the centre of the universe for the emperors who lived inside it; standing in its main courtyard, with the crowd thinning out toward the far pavilions, you feel the scale of what was built and why. The Great Wall, an hour from the city, predates even that.

### Day 17: Tiananmen & the Forbidden City

The **Forbidden City** requires advance ticket booking — the daily visitor cap fills weeks ahead during high season. Enter from Tiananmen Gate to the south and walk the full axis north to Jingshan Park, where the hill at its centre gives you the classic overhead view of the palace complex's yellow-tiled roofs stretching away in every direction.

**Tiananmen Square** is either the first or last thing you visit depending on your schedule and the gate configuration on the day. The political weight of the space is something every visitor processes differently; it is enormous, and the portrait of Mao above the gate watching over it is larger than it looks in photographs.

### Day 18: Great Wall & Hutongs

Go to the **Mutianyu section** of the Great Wall — a 2-hour drive or direct bus from the city. It is better-preserved than Badaling, less crowded, and the toboggan descent from the wall back to the valley is an excellent way to end the morning.

Return to the city by mid-afternoon and spend the evening walking the **Nanluoguxiang and Wudaoying** hutong neighbourhoods — the old alleyway districts of Beijing where the residential fabric of the old city survives. Small restaurants, independent coffee shops, dumpling joints, and courtyard bars line the lanes. Have Peking duck for your last dinner: **Da Dong Roast Duck** is the most consistent, but any of the old established places will do it justice.

### Hotels

- **The Opposite House** — Design hotel in Sanlitun; understated but excellent
- **Hotel Cote Cour SL** — Mid-range boutique in a converted courtyard in the Dongcheng district; atmospheric

### Restaurant Recommendations

- **Da Dong Roast Duck** — The definitive version; book ahead
- **Baoyuan Dumplings** — Famous for coloured dumplings, long queues, worth the wait
- **Mao Mao Chong Bar** — A hutong bar worth finding for cocktails and atmosphere

## General Travel Tips

### Transportation

China's high-speed rail is one of the world's great transport systems. Trains are punctual, clean, and fast — the Beijing to Shanghai run covers 1,300km in 4.5 hours. All you need is your passport to book and board. Tickets sell out on popular routes during holidays; book as early as possible via Trip.com.

**DiDi** is the Chinese equivalent of Uber and works on a standard app. Download and register before you travel. Drivers rarely speak English but the in-app map communication usually suffices.

### Payment

Set up WeChat Pay or Alipay before you go — link a foreign Visa or Mastercard. Without one of these, paying for food, metro tickets, and shopping in most Chinese cities becomes difficult. Some high-end hotels and tourist sites still accept cards directly, but don't rely on it.

### Language

Mandarin Pinyin basics (hello, thank you, where is, how much) go a long way. The translation features inside WeChat are useful for menus. Google Translate's camera function works well in China once you have a VPN running.

### Best Time to Visit

| Season | Time | Weather | Notes |
|--------|------|---------|-------|
| **Spring** | March–May | Mild, some rain | Flowers, green landscapes; Zhangjiajie mist at its best |
| **Summer** | June–August | Hot and humid | Busy, more afternoon storms; cheaper accommodation |
| **Autumn** | September–November | Clear, cool | Best overall conditions; peak season at the Wall |
| **Winter** | December–February | Cold in the north | Fewer crowds; Pingyao and Beijing quieter but cold; south remains mild |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need a visa for China?**
Most western passport holders need a visa in advance. The process is straightforward via the Chinese embassy or consulate in your home country. Many nationalities now qualify for visa-free entry for up to 30 days — check the current rules for your passport well before booking.

**Is China safe to travel independently?**
Extremely safe for tourists in terms of street crime. The main practical challenges are the language barrier and the Great Firewall. With a VPN, offline maps (Maps.me or Apple Maps downloaded offline), and a working payment app, independent travel is very manageable.

**How do I get around the Great Firewall?**
Install a reliable VPN before you arrive. Astrill and ExpressVPN are the two most consistent options. Free VPNs rarely work inside China.

**Should I take the high-speed train or fly?**
For most legs on this itinerary, both are viable. The HST is generally preferable for journeys under 5 hours — no security theatre, city-centre stations, and the ride itself is pleasant. Fly when the distance makes the train impractical (Guilin–Zhangjiajie, Chongqing–Xi'an on this route).

**How do I buy train tickets?**
Trip.com is the easiest option for foreigners — it accepts international cards and has an English interface. The official 12306 app also works but requires a Chinese phone number to set up. Book early for popular routes, especially during national holidays.

## Final Thoughts

The south-to-north route works because it gives you a through-line: the tropics of Guangdong, the karst valleys of Guangxi, the surreal cliffs of Hunan, the spice-blasted heat of Sichuan, the ancient imperial splendour of Shaanxi, and finally the accumulated weight of five thousand years of continuous civilisation in Beijing.

China is not an easy country to visit, and it's not trying to be. The rewards are proportional to the effort: the landscapes, the food, the historical depth, and the sheer scale of it all are unlike anything else on earth.

**Happy travels.**

Chongqing is one of those cities that nobody talks about and everyone should visit. It's a super-modern megacity built vertically into misty mountain cliffs, and absolutely packed with things that will make you stop and say: wait, what? A monorail that runs through the middle of an apartment building. Beautiful ancient towns in the middle of a city full of skyscrapers. A skyline full of skyscrapers that all light up with giant screens on them. A drone show over the river with over 1,000 drones. It's a surreal, cyberpunk dream that you can immerse youself in.

We've visited twice and both times were incredible. This is a guide to the highlights, practical things to sort before you arrive, how to get around, and the experiences that made Chongqing one of the most memorable stops on our trip.

## Before You Arrive: Apps You Need

WeChat and Alipay are non-negotiable. Without these two apps, daily life in Chongqing (and China in general) becomes significantly harder. Download and set them up before you land. The others we recommend will make your life a lot easier too.

### WeChat

[iOS Download](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/wechat/id414478124) | [Android Download](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.tencent.mm&pcampaignid=web_share)

WeChat is China's all-purpose super-app; messaging, payments, maps, restaurant menus, and more. You will need it to communicate with locals and tours, scan QR codes at restaurants to view menus, and access websites that don't exist on Western internet. Set it up with your phone number before you arrive. You can also add a payment method so you can use Wexin Pay, which is the payment system built into WeChat.

### Alipay
[iOS Download](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/alipay-SIMplify-your-life/id333206289) | [Android Download](https://play.google.com/store/search?q=alipay&c=apps&hl=en)

Alipay is the dominant payment app in China. Cash is rarely accepted, and many vendors, transit systems, and tourist attractions prefer or exclusively use mobile payment. Alipay now has a Tourist Edition specifically for foreign visitors that lets you add an international credit or debit card without needing a Chinese bank account. To pay, you scan the QR code at the register and confirm the amount of money on your phone. The payment is instant and the shopkeeper gets a notification on their end.

**Tip:** Both apps require a phone number for verification. Make sure yours is set up and functional before you land. Hotel wifi is fine for setup, but having data on arrival helps.

### Amap
[iOS Download](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/amap-global/id461703208) | [Android Download](https://play.google.com/store/search?q=amap&c=apps&hl=en)

Google Maps does not work in China. Amap is the best alternative for navigation, especially for public transit. It has an English mode and is fairly intuitive once you get used to it. You can also use it to find nearby restaurants, shops, and attractions. Apple maps works reasonably well for basic navigation, but Amap is better for transit directions and local points of interest.

### Didi
[iOS Download](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/didi-china-ride-hailing/id554499054) | [Android Download](https://play.google.com/store/search?q=didi%20china&c=apps&hl=en)

Didi is the Chinese equivalent of Uber and is widely used for taxis and private car rides. It's a convenient option for getting around if you don't want to navigate the metro or if you're traveling late at night when public transit is less frequent. The app has an English interface and accepts international payment methods. Didi has a separate app for rides in China vs the rest of the world.

### Trip.com
[iOS Download](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/trip-com-book-flights-hotels/id681752345) | [Android Download](https://play.google.com/store/search?q=trip.com&c=apps&hl=en)

Trip.com is an essential app for booking tours, hotels, transportation, and attractions. Trip is a Chinese company, so it has the best availability of activities but also great english support given it is used internationally. You can also use it to book your airport transfer or local transport if you prefer not to navigate Chinese apps. It's the best way to buy tickets for long distance bullet or sleeper trains.

## Wifi

Wifi is ubiquitous around China, almost every restaurant, hotel, public space will have it, but it is censored. It's important to note that to access it you will need to provide a phone number that can receive SMS, as nearly every wifi network asks for it as a form of identity. It's non-negotiable, and you'll find many other services also require SMS verification so if your home SIM can't receive texts abroad for free, you may struggle. A eSIM solves most of these problems

## Before You Arrive: Money

While cash is accepted almost everywhere, it is seldom expected. Most places may struggle to give you change as it is just not used anymore. Over a three week trip, we used it on two occasions. When we returned, we didn't bother withdrawing any. If you do want some cash, ICBC is your best bet for an ATM that accepts foreign cards. Visa, MasterCard and Amex are not widely accepted, since China uses its own network - Unionpay.

## Getting Around: The Metro

Chongqing has an excellent metro system and it is by far the easiest and cheapest way to navigate the city (2RMB/$0.40 per ride). The lines cover all the major tourist areas and the trains are clean, air-conditioned, and frequent. All metro signage is in both Chinese and English, so finding your way around is very manageable even without being able to read or speak Chinese. All station names and signs are transliterated into phonetic English (e.g., Liziba, Jiefangbei, Linjiangmen), which makes them easy to follow on a map.

The easiest way to pay for a subway fare is to use the transit QR code in Alipay, which is what we did. Unlike Shanghai, you can't tap on and off with a credit card. You can buy tickets at each station but this is a lot less convenient.

## Activities

There are a lot of things to do in Chongqing, but these are the ones that stood out as unique and memorable. It can be tricky to find information about these activites on Western internet, so I've included details on how to book and what to expect.

## HongYa Cave (洪崖洞 — HongYaDong)

HongYa Cave is one of Chongqing's most iconic landmarks and absolutely worth visiting, ideally in the evening when it comes to life. Built into a cliff face overlooking the Jialing River, it's an 11-storey complex of traditional Bayu-style stilt houses (diaojiaolou) packed with restaurants, bars, teahouses, and shops. At night, thousands of red lanterns glow from every level and the entire structure reflects on the river below. It's genuinely stunning.

Despite its touristy reputation, it's a legitimately beautiful place and worth at least an hour or two of wandering. Walk up through the levels, grab a hotpot meal, and make your way down to the riverside boardwalk for the best view looking back at the complex. There is a maze of souvenir shops and food vendors, plenty of free samples, and it is quite magical to amble around.

The best exterior view is from the **QianSIMen Bridge** (just across the river), or from the riverside walkway below. If you want the famous photo of the whole building lit up at night, cross the bridge and look back.

**Getting there:** Linjiangmen Station (Line 2), 5-minute walk.

### Hanfu Photoshoot at Hongya Cave

One of the best things you can do at HongYa Cave is rent a hanfu, traditional Chinese clothing, and have a professional photoshoot in front of the lit-up complex. Rental shops are located inside and nearby the Hongya Cave complex, and most offer a full package: outfit selection, hair styling, accessories, and a photographer for an hour or two around the complex.

This is enormously popular with Chinese tourists and visitors alike, and it's a genuinely fun experience. The combination of the traditional costume with the glowing lanterns and stilt-house backdrop makes for spectacular photos. Prices vary depending on the package, but expect to pay roughly ¥400–700 CNY per person including the photographer. Most shops have some English and will walk you through the options. We booked through [Hugh Chongqing Tours](https://hughchongqing.com/) and just messaged him on WhatsApp.

Walking aroung HongYa Cave you'll be approached by many people with iPads offering to sell you the experience, and if you go down to level 3 you'll find all of their stores with the myriad costume options. Don't expect a lot of English to be spoken, but we got by with a translator app.

## The Subway That Goes Through a Building — Liziba Station (李子坝站)

This is one of Chongqing's most famous quirks and it deserves a spot on your itinerary. Liziba Station on Line 2 is built directly into floors 6 through 8 of a residential apartment building. The train literally passes through the building every few minutes, so you're never waiting long for a photo opportunity. 

This isn't a gimmick or a tourist attraction by design, it's the result of Chongqing's extreme topography. The city is so hilly that standard at-grade or underground metro lines are often impossible, so engineers built elevated track wherever the terrain allowed, including, in this case, straight through an existing building.

The best way to see it is from the street below, where you can watch the trains disappear into and emerge from the building. There's a viewing platform and a small crowd of onlookers at most times of day.

**Getting there:** Liziba Station (Line 2) — you're already there. Get off, exit, and look up.

## Cable Car Over the Yangtze River

Chongqing has a cable car (长江索道) that crosses the Yangtze River, running between the Yuzhong Peninsula and the south bank of the river, suspended high above the water with views of the city skyline in both directions. It's not a scenic mountain cable car — it's a commuter cable car, originally built in 1987 to connect two parts of the city before metro lines existed. It's now something of a tourist institution, but locals still use it too.

The ride takes a few minutes each way and the views are excellent — you get a good perspective on the sheer scale of the riverbanks and the density of the city stacked up on both sides. It's a short experience but a memorable one.

**Getting there:** Xinhua Lu Station (Line 1 or Line 6), then walk to the cable car terminal. The south bank terminal is a short walk from Wangfujing Station.

**Tickets:** Around ¥10 CNY per person one-way, purchased at the terminal. Alipay accepted.

**Tip:** The cable car closes periodically for maintenance. Check ahead if you're making a special trip.

## The 1941 Immersive Show

The 1941 show is an immersive theatrical experience set in wartime Chongqing. The city served as the provisional capital of China during the Second Sino-Japanese War and was heavily bombed by Japanese forces throughout the early 1940s. This performance brings that era to life through a combination of live actors, elaborate sets, lighting, and special effects spread across multiple rooms and spaces that the audience moves through.

It's a unique experience and unlike anything you'll find at most tourist destinations. The production values are high and even without fluent Mandarin, the visual storytelling carries the narrative well. It runs for around 60–90 minutes and is housed in a dedicated venue designed specifically for the show.

Free translator headsets are available for many foreign languages. They don't cover all of the dialogue, but they definitely helped us get the gist of what was happening.

**Booking:** Book tickets in advance through the venue's WeChat account or a third-party booking platform. Tickets are popular and can sell out, particularly on weekends.

**Tip:** The experience is in Chinese, but the theatrical nature of it makes it accessible even without language. Go in knowing the basic historical context of Chongqing in WWII and you'll get more out of it.

## Ciqikou Ancient Town (磁器口)

Right next to the theature is Ciqikou, a preserved ancient town on the banks of the Jialing River in the Shapingba district, dating back over 1,700 years. It's a classic old-town experience: narrow cobblestone alleys, traditional wooden architecture, incense smoke, and an enormous density of food stalls selling everything from spicy rabbit heads to crispy mochi and much more.

It can feel crowded on weekends, but it's worth visiting for the architecture and the food alone. Pick up some Chongqing-style street food as you walk — the stinky tofu and glutinous rice cakes are particularly good — and explore the side streets off the main drag which are quieter and more atmospheric.

There's also a small river beach area you can access from the lower end of the town, which is a nice spot to slow down after the bustle of the main alley.

**Getting there:** Ciqikou Station (Line 1), 5-minute walk.

**Best time to visit:** Weekday mornings are significantly less crowded. Avoid Sunday afternoons.

## Drone Show Over the River

Every Saturday evening, Chongqing puts on a large-scale drone show over the river — hundreds of illuminated drones forming shapes, patterns, and scenes in the night sky above the Yangtze. It's the kind of spectacle that photographs badly but looks incredible in person. 

The start time varies depending on the season from 8:30pm to 9pm, so check with your hotel or google before you go to avoid disappointment.

The show typically runs for 10–15 minutes and is best viewed from the riverfront promenade or from the viewing area near Chaotianmen (朝天门), where the Jialing and Yangtze rivers meet. Check the local schedule as timing can vary by season and day of week.

The show is very popular, and it is in a corner of the city without much public transport (rare for China!). The promenade fills up with locals and the streets are filled with taxis all struggling to get through the narrow streets to the same place. We took a taxi most of the way but still ended up walking for 20 minutes to find a spot. We had to walk 30 minutes back to the train station as there were no buses or Didis available.

**Getting there:** Chaotianmen Square is a short walk from Xiaoshizi Station (Line 1 or Line 6).

**Tip:** Arrive 20–30 minutes early to get a good spot on the promenade. We found a comfy seat in one of the many bars on the lower promenade, ordered a beer and sat back to watch the show.

## Raffles City Rooftop — The Crystal

The Raffles City complex in Chongqing is a striking architectural project: eight towers connected at the top by a sky bridge called The Crystal, suspended 250 metres above the city. The observation deck at the top offers 360-degree views of Chongqing, the rivers, and the mountains beyond.

Beyond the observation deck, The Crystal has several restaurants and bars at height, making it a great option for a memorable dinner or sundowner with a view. There is a bar area that's walk-in, though restaurant reservations are recommended for peak times.

**Getting there:** Chaotianmen Station (Line 1).

**Observation deck tickets:** Around ¥150 CNY. Book in advance or at the tower. Note: the ticket also gives access to a light and projection show on the bridge itself.

**Tip:** The viewing platform is best for sunset views as it faces west. We dined at the Shua restaurant which, unbeknownst to us, faces east, so has no view of the sunset, but was still a novel experience. The food was pricey, but very good.

## Local Neighbourhood Tour

The best thing you can do in Chongqing outside the main attractions is get lost in one of its residential neighbourhoods. The city's dramatic topography means streets dead-end into staircases, stairways suddenly open into elevated plazas, and what looks like a basement level from one street is a rooftop from the next. Navigation is genuinely disorienting in the best way.

We would highly recommend [Hugh Chongqing Tours](https://hughchongqing.com/). He is a Chongqing local and offers a variety of private tours at a very reasonable price. We did the [local neighbourhoods walking tour](https://hughchongqing.com/city-walking-tours/walking-tour-local-neighborhoods/) and loved the experience. Our guide was a local from one of the nearby neighbourhoods and gave us really great insights and showed us hidden places we would have never found on our own. 


## Longmenhao Old Street (龙门浩老街)

Longmenhao is a restored historical district on the south bank of the Yangtze, directly across the river from the Chaotianmen peninsula. It's smaller than Ciqikou, and the restoration has been done with a lighter touch — there are fewer food stalls and more actual history. But if you're staying on the peninsula, this is much closer - only one stop on the metro, just across the river to the east.

The area covers several levels of old stone streets, traditional courtyards, and heritage buildings that tell the story of Chongqing's development as a river trade city. Several of the buildings have been converted into small cafes, art studios, and galleries. It's a genuinely pleasant place to spend an afternoon, especially if you cross via the cable car or take a scenic walk over one of the river bridges.

**Getting there:** Take the metro to Haizibian Station (Line 3) or take a ferry across from Chaotianmen.

**Tip:** Combine this with a visit to the south bank riverfront promenade for excellent skyline views looking back towards the CBD.

## Where to Stay

### The Westin Chongqing Liberation Square

The Westin is excellently located in the heart of the Jiefangbei (Liberation Square) CBD, within walking distance of Hongya Cave, the Raffles City complex, and the Chaotianmen waterfront. It's a solid luxury option with a large pool, comfortable rooms, and the usual Westin reliability.

Jiefangbei is the best base in Chongqing for first-time visitors. You're close to the metro, the riverside, and the main attractions, and the neighbourhood has excellent food options at every price point; from basement noodle stalls to rooftop restaurants.

**Getting there:** Jiefangbei Station (Line 1 or Line 6).

## Practical Tips

**Language:** English is not widely spoken outside hotel lobbies and major tourist attractions. Having a translation app on your phone (Google Translate works fine with an eSIM, or use the built-in translation in WeChat) will get you far. People are generally patient and helpful if you're clearly making an effort.

**Payment:** Cash is not widly used so getting change can be problematic. If you prefer to have some cash ICBC is your best bet for an ATM that accepts foreign cards. Visa, MasterCard and Amex are not widely accepted, since China uses its own network - Unionpay.

**Hotpot:** Chongqing is the birthplace of mala hotpot, and you owe it to yourself to eat it while you're here. The local version is significantly spicier than anything you'll find elsewhere. Start mild if you're heat-sensitive and work up. Your lips will go numb. This is normal and correct.

**Spice warning:** Chongqing food is heavily spiced with both chilli and Sichuan peppercorn. If you have any spice sensitivity, communicate it clearly when ordering. The default heat level at most local restaurants is high.

## Final Thoughts

Chongqing rewards curiosity. It's not a city that sells itself neatly. The attractions are scattered, the geography is confusing, and a lot of what makes it extraordinary is best discovered by walking around with no plan. Give yourself at least three or four full days. Sort your apps before you arrive, get on the metro, and start walking uphill. You'll find something worth stopping for within five minutes.

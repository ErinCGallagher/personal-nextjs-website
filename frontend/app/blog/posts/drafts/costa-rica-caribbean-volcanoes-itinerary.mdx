Most Costa Rica itineraries follow a familiar circuit: San José to La Fortuna, Monteverde, and one of the Pacific beaches. That loop exists for good reason — the highlights are exceptional. But this itinerary goes somewhere different first. It starts on the Caribbean coast, which operates on a completely separate rhythm: Afro-Caribbean culture, reggae, reef-fringed beaches, and jungle so dense you can only reach parts of it by boat. From there it cuts through the volcanic highlands before arriving at Arenal and Monteverde for a finish that earns its place as the best cloud forest hiking in Central America.

This is a more logistically involved route — 10 to 14 days gives you room to do it without rushing. The payoff is seeing sides of Costa Rica that most travellers never reach.

## Day 1: San José to Tortuguero (Travel Day)

Tortuguero is one of those places that earns its reputation precisely because getting there requires effort. There are no roads into the village — you drive to a river dock and take a boat the rest of the way through the jungle canals. Plan for a full travel day.

The drive from San José to La Pavona, the main departure point for boats, takes around six hours, mostly on paved roads heading northeast through the Caribbean lowlands. The landscape shifts gradually as you gain altitude and then descend: from city sprawl to coffee plantations to banana operations and finally to the dense, humid Caribbean plain. Have breakfast before you leave San José — there are few stops worth making along the route.

At La Pavona, you leave the car in a supervised car park and board a covered motorboat for the final leg to Tortuguero. The boat transfer takes one to two hours through a network of jungle canals, and this stretch is genuinely spectacular even before you've done anything else on the trip. The waterways are narrow in places — overhanging trees close overhead, the water is dark and still, and the sounds of the jungle replace the sound of engines. Along the banks you can spot caimans sunning on logs, green basilisk lizards running across the water surface, great blue herons standing in the shallows, anhingas drying their wings, and kingfishers shooting between branches. Howler monkeys are often heard before they're seen. This is your introduction to Tortuguero: wild, wet, and unlike anywhere else in Costa Rica.

Arrive at the village in the late afternoon, check in, and rest. You'll want energy for the next two days.

## Days 2–3 (or 2–4): Tortuguero

Tortuguero is a small Caribbean village of a few hundred people, without cars, sitting between the jungle canals and the sea. There are a handful of restaurants, souvenir shops, and tour operators along the main path through town — it takes about ten minutes to walk end to end. The pace is slow, which suits the place perfectly.

### Guided Rainforest Walk

The rainforest surrounding Tortuguero is part of Tortuguero National Park, one of Costa Rica's most biodiverse protected areas. A guided walk through the primary jungle is a full sensory experience: the air is thick and warm, the canopy is dense overhead, and the forest floor is loud with insects and birds. Guides know where to look — and you won't spot half of what's here without one.

Common sightings on guided walks include: Central American spider monkeys and howler monkeys in the canopy, two- and three-toed sloths hanging in cecropia trees, poison dart frogs (particularly the red-and-blue Dendrobates pumilio, known locally as the strawberry frog), green and black iguanas, land crabs, and an extraordinary range of birds including toucans, parrots, and the great green macaw. Guides typically charge $25–$40 USD per person for a two-to-three-hour walk and are found through your lodge or at the national park entrance.

Tortuguero National Park entrance fee: approximately $15 USD per person.

### Sea Turtle Watching

Tortuguero is one of the most important sea turtle nesting sites in the Western Hemisphere. Green turtles are the primary species, nesting from July through October — the peak is August and September. Leatherback turtles nest earlier in the year, from March through July. Outside these windows, turtle watching is not available.

Nightly tours operate under strict regulations: all visits must be accompanied by a licensed guide, group sizes are kept small, flash photography and phone lights are prohibited (they disorient the turtles), and visitors must wait in designated areas until a nesting turtle has been located by rangers on the beach. The whole experience typically takes two to three hours, including the walk out. Despite the restrictions, watching a leatherback the size of a coffee table haul herself up the beach and lay a clutch of eggs is one of the more remarkable things you can witness in nature.

Book turtle tours through your lodge or with a registered guide in the village. Cost: approximately $25–$35 USD per person. Departure is after 8:00 PM.

**⭐ Tip:** Turtle tour spots fill up quickly in peak season (August–September). Book at least a day ahead, ideally before you arrive.

### Guided Canal Kayaking

Tortuguero's canal network extends for kilometres in both directions from the village, and exploring it by kayak gets you closer to the wildlife than any motorboat can. The canals are perfectly flat — no current, no white water — so this is accessible to anyone comfortable in a kayak. Early morning is the best time: the light is soft, the birds are active, and the caiman are often visible on the banks.

On a good morning you can expect to see spectacled caimans at close range, black-necked stilts, boat-billed herons, northern jacanas walking across lily pads, river otters, and — with luck — manatees in the deeper channels. Kayak tours typically run two to three hours and cost around $30–$45 USD per person, including equipment and a guide. Operators in the village rent kayaks independently for around $10–$15 USD per hour if you prefer to go at your own pace.

### Tortuguero Village & Canal Boat Tours

The village itself is worth a wander — it's genuinely lived-in rather than built for tourists, and the main canal-side path is pleasant in the evenings. Several operators offer half-day motorboat tours of the wider canal network, which covers more ground than kayaking and is better suited for birdwatching with binoculars. These tours cost approximately $20–$30 USD per person and include a guide.

## Days 4–5 (or 5–6): Puerto Viejo de Talamanca

The drive from Tortuguero to Puerto Viejo requires retracing your boat journey first — back to La Pavona by water, then south and east by road through Limón, the main port city on Costa Rica's Caribbean coast. The total road distance is around 170 kilometres and takes approximately four hours, depending on traffic through Limón.

The road south of Limón toward Puerto Viejo (Route 36) runs along the coast and is in reasonable condition, though sections can deteriorate after heavy rain. Drive carefully on any stretches of unpaved road; travel speeds are slow by necessity. Limón itself is a working port city and worth a brief stop for petrol and food if you need it, but it is not a tourist destination.

Puerto Viejo, when you arrive, is immediately its own thing. The pace of life here has nothing to do with the rest of Costa Rica. Afro-Caribbean culture — descended from Jamaican migrants who came to build the railway in the late 1800s — defines the town: the music playing from open bars is reggae and dancehall, the food is cooked with coconut milk, and the pace is deeply unhurried. It is one of the most distinctive towns in the country.

### Where to Stay in Puerto Viejo de Talamanca

| Option | Price per person per night |
|--------|---------------------------|
| **Budget** — Rocking J's, legendary backpacker hostel with hammock camping and dorm beds, right on the beach | ~$15–$30 USD |
| **Mid-range** — Hotel Cashew Hill Jungle Lodge, quiet hillside rooms with views, good breakfast, short walk to the village | ~$60–$90 USD |
| **High-end** — Samasati Nature Retreat, yoga retreat on a hillside with rainforest views and excellent restaurant, very peaceful | ~$150–$220 USD |

### Caribbean Food

Eating in Puerto Viejo is one of the pleasures of this section of the trip. Soda Tamara and Soda Miss Sam are two long-standing local sodas (casual Costa Rican restaurants) serving Caribbean-style rice and beans cooked in coconut milk — a completely different dish from the gallo pinto on the Pacific side, richer and more aromatic. Rondon is the dish most associated with this coast: a thick coconut milk stew cooked with whatever fish, tubers, and vegetables are available that day.

**Caribbean patties** are the essential street food of Puerto Viejo — flaky pastry filled with spiced beef or vegetables, baked golden, and eaten hot. You will find patties at several spots in town; the ones sold fresh from the bakery near the main street are the ones to hunt down. They cost around $1–$2 USD each.

### Cahuita National Park

Cahuita is a 15-minute drive north of Puerto Viejo, and one of the most accessible coral reef snorkelling sites in Costa Rica. Entry operates on an honour-system donation at the Cahuita village entrance (suggested $5 USD per person) or free with registration at the Puerto Vargas entrance at the park's southern end.

The coastal trail runs 9 kilometres from Cahuita village to Puerto Vargas, passing through primary forest right along the beach. It is flat, easy, and alive with wildlife: white-faced capuchin monkeys are common along the trail and will approach at close range, three-toed sloths hang in the trees above the path, coatis forage in the leaf litter, and raccoons are sometimes spotted near the tideline.

**Playa Blanca**, the beach at the Cahuita village entrance, is a long arc of white sand with palm trees at the back — this is the swimming beach, and it is beautiful. Snorkelling is best at the reef offshore from Playa Blanca and Puerto Vargas when visibility is good — the reef shelters brain coral, parrotfish, angelfish, and occasional nurse sharks resting on the bottom. Visibility is variable and best in September and October after the rainy season clears sediment. Snorkel gear can be hired in Cahuita village for around $10 USD per person.

### Manzanillo National Wildlife Refuge

Manzanillo is 12 kilometres southeast of Puerto Viejo along a mostly paved coastal road, and the contrast with Cahuita is significant — this is quieter, less visited, and feels more remote. The refuge protects a stretch of coastline, reef, and primary forest. Bottle-nosed dolphins are regularly spotted in the bay, and the snorkelling over the reef here is considered by many to be the best on this stretch of coast.

A local guide from the village of Manzanillo can take you into the forest along the coastal trail, where wildlife sightings include all four of Costa Rica's monkey species, sloths, and a remarkable density of birds including the chestnut-mandibled toucan. Water taxis from the beach can reach more isolated reef sections for snorkelling. Guided snorkel tours from Puerto Viejo to Manzanillo cost approximately $40–$60 USD per person; entry to the refuge itself is free.

### Punta Uva Beach

Halfway between Puerto Viejo and Manzanillo, Punta Uva is arguably the most beautiful beach on this stretch of coast — a sheltered, crescent-shaped bay with clear water and no development to speak of. It is ideal for a quiet morning swim. No entrance fee.

### Bribri Cacao Tour

One of the most worthwhile experiences near Puerto Viejo is a cacao tour with the Bribri indigenous community in the Talamanca mountains inland from the coast. The Bribri have cultivated cacao for centuries as a ceremonial and food crop, and their tours involve the full process from harvesting the pod to fermenting, roasting, and grinding the beans into a traditional chocolate drink. Several operators run these tours, typically combined with a short boat ride up the Yorkin or Telire river. Cost: approximately $50–$80 USD per person for a half-day. Tours can be booked through Terraventuras in Puerto Viejo or directly with community operators.

**⭐ Tip:** The Bribri cacao tour is consistently one of the highest-rated experiences in this region. If your schedule allows only one half-day activity in Puerto Viejo, prioritise this one.

### Nightlife

Puerto Viejo is one of the few places in Costa Rica with a genuine bar scene. Stanford's Caribbean Restaurant, Johnny's Place, and the cluster of bars around the main road get going after 10:00 PM on weekends. The music is almost exclusively reggae and dancehall. It is a proper night out if you're in the mood for it.

## Day 6 (or 7): Puerto Viejo to Ciudad Quesada — Waterfall Drive

Ciudad Quesada (also called San Carlos) is not a tourist destination. It is an agricultural and commercial hub in the northern Central Valley, and the reason you are stopping here is entirely practical: it puts you within easy reach of Juan Castro Blanco National Park for an early start the following morning, and cuts a very long drive into two manageable days.

Rather than taking the direct highway route through Braulio Carrillo National Park, this day is worth stretching into a full scenic drive via the Poás highlands, where two spectacular waterfalls sit just a few kilometres apart on a road that qualifies as a highlight in its own right. The route adds time but delivers highland dairy farm country, cloud forest, and views toward Poás Volcano — a completely different landscape from the Caribbean lowlands you are leaving behind. Start early from Puerto Viejo to give yourself time at both waterfalls before arriving in Quesada for the night.

### La Paz Waterfall Gardens

La Paz Waterfall Gardens sits near Varablanca on the highland road between Alajuela and La Fortuna, roughly three hours from Puerto Viejo. It is a private wildlife sanctuary and waterfall park set on the slopes of Poás Volcano — a well-run attraction that manages to feel genuinely immersive rather than manufactured.

The site includes five waterfalls on the Río La Paz, connected by a trail through cloud forest that descends steeply into a gorge. The largest waterfall is around 37 metres. Beyond the waterfalls, the complex holds a wildlife sanctuary where rescued animals — sloths, toucans, poison dart frogs, snakes, pumas, and jaguars in large enclosures — are kept in good conditions while being rehabilitated or permanently cared for. The hummingbird garden, with over 26 species at feeders, is one of the best in Costa Rica.

Allow two to three hours here. Entry: approximately $55 USD per adult. Open 8:00 AM to 5:00 PM.

### Catarata del Toro

About 20 kilometres from La Paz via the scenic highland road through Bajos del Toro, Catarata del Toro is a dramatically different experience. A 90-metre waterfall drops straight into an ancient volcanic crater — the falls are among the highest in Costa Rica, and the crater pool at the base is a perfect circle of dark water enclosed by near-vertical walls draped in ferns and mosses.

Access involves a 15–20 minute descent through private land; the owners have built a good path with handrails for the steeper sections. The final viewpoint at the base of the crater is genuinely arresting — the waterfall comes off the rim above you and the sound fills the crater. The road between La Paz and Bajos del Toro winds through pasture and cloud forest on unpaved highland roads with views in every direction on clear days. This stretch is the scenic drive the user is referring to — unhurried and beautiful. A vehicle with some clearance is recommended; the unpaved sections after rain can be slippery.

Entry: approximately $12–$15 USD per adult.

From Bajos del Toro, continue north through Zarcero to Ciudad Quesada — approximately 45 minutes on mostly paved roads.

Ciudad Quesada has good services: petrol stations, supermarkets, pharmacies, ATMs, and a range of inexpensive restaurants and sodas. It is a functioning mid-sized Costa Rican city, pleasant without being remarkable, and perfectly comfortable for a night. Eat at any of the local sodas around the central park — the casado (a plate of rice, beans, salad, and protein) is cheap and filling.

### Where to Stay in Ciudad Quesada

| Option | Price per person per night |
|--------|---------------------------|
| **Budget** — Hotel El Retiro, clean and central with parking, no frills | ~$20–$30 USD |
| **Mid-range** — Hotel La Central, comfortable rooms with hot water and a good restaurant attached | ~$35–$55 USD |

## Day 7 (or 8): Juan Castro Blanco National Park

Juan Castro Blanco is one of the least-visited national parks in Costa Rica, which is exactly what makes it worth going to. The park protects primary cloud forest and secondary growth on the slopes below the Platanar and Poás volcanoes at altitudes between 900 and 2,267 metres. There are rivers, waterfalls, and on clear days, views toward Poás. The wildlife is the same as you would expect from any cloud forest in Costa Rica — resplendent quetzals are present but elusive, trogons, toucans, and a wide variety of highland birds are reliable, and mammals including pumas, tapirs, and ocelots inhabit the park, though sightings of the larger species are uncommon.

The park is administered by SINAC (Costa Rica's national conservation system) and receives relatively few visitors. Entrance costs approximately $10 USD per person. It is located roughly 30 kilometres west of Ciudad Quesada near the town of Ciudad Quesada; the access road from Aguas Zarcas is unpaved and may require a vehicle with clearance after rain.

**Note:** Juan Castro Blanco is not heavily documented in English, and trail maps are limited. The information below is based on what is known of the park's trail network, but conditions and trail availability should be confirmed with the park rangers at the entrance station before setting out.

### Trail Options at Juan Castro Blanco

**Sendero Principal (Main Trail)** — approximately 5 km one way, moderate difficulty. The park's primary access trail follows the Río Platanar upstream through dense primary forest. The river crossings are straightforward in dry conditions but require care after heavy rain. This trail takes you into the interior of the park, away from the entrance, with the best chance of encountering wildlife in the quieter sections. Allow three to four hours return.

**Sendero La Catarata (Waterfall Trail)** — approximately 2 km one way, easy to moderate. A shorter loop from the park entrance that leads to a waterfall on one of the park's rivers. This is a good option if you have limited time or want a shorter introduction to the forest before pushing on to La Fortuna.

**Upper Forest Access (toward Platanar slopes)** — more demanding, longer, and less defined. If the rangers at the entrance confirm access, pushing higher into the park rewards you with cloud forest more typical of the altitudes above 1,500 metres — mossy trunks, bromeliads, and a noticeably different feel from the lower sections. This is not a loop; turn back when you've had enough. Allow a full day.

Drive to La Fortuna after hiking — it takes roughly 1.5 hours from the park entrance.

## Days 8–9 (or 9–10): La Fortuna & Arenal Volcano

La Fortuna is Costa Rica's most famous inland destination, built around the view of Arenal — a near-perfect volcanic cone that dominates the eastern skyline. The volcano's last major eruption cycle ran from 1968 to 2010; it is currently in a resting phase, which means no lava flows, but the geothermal activity in the surrounding land is very much alive. Hot springs, fumaroles, and a landscape defined by old lava fields make this one of the most dramatic environments in Central America.

The drive from Juan Castro Blanco takes approximately 1.5 hours northwest on Route 4.

### Where to Stay in La Fortuna

| Option | Price per person per night |
|--------|---------------------------|
| **Budget** — Arenal Backpackers Resort, good facilities, pool, views of the volcano from the grounds | ~$25–$40 USD |
| **Mid-range** — Lost Iguana Resort, on the slopes of the volcano with excellent views, pool, and a good restaurant | ~$120–$175 USD |
| **High-end** — Tabacón Grand Spa Thermal Resort, the most famous hot springs property in Costa Rica, thermal river running through the grounds, multiple pools | ~$280–$400 USD |

### Arenal Volcano Hike — Arenal Volcano National Park

Arenal Volcano National Park is the starting point for any serious hiking in this area. The park entrance fee is approximately $18 USD per person.

**Sendero Los Tucanes** (Toucan Trail) — a 4.3 km loop through secondary forest and old lava fields from the 1968 eruption. The hardened lava is colonised by pioneer vegetation at its edges, and the contrast between bare black rock and living forest is striking. Toucans, coatis, and howler monkeys are common. This trail can be done independently in two to three hours.

**Sendero Las Coladas** (Lava Flow Trail) — a shorter, more dramatic trail that takes you directly across the 1992 lava flow and up toward the base of the volcano cone. Views of the cone are best from the upper sections. This trail requires more fitness and is best done in the morning before cloud covers the summit. Hire a guide for the upper sections: approximately $30–$50 USD per person through operators in La Fortuna town.

Guides can be arranged in town or through your lodge. For the best wildlife sightings, early morning (before 7:00 AM) is the most productive time in the park.

### Ecotermales Hot Springs

The geothermal hot springs around La Fortuna are a legitimate natural phenomenon and not to be missed. Ecotermales Fortuna is one of the better options: a series of natural pools of varying temperatures set in riverside gardens, kept deliberately uncrowded with admission caps. Entry costs approximately $45 USD per person and is best reserved in the evening after hiking, when soaking in a 38°C pool with a jungle canopy overhead is a very good way to end the day. Book in advance as slots sell out.

Tabacón Resort operates the most famous hot springs in the area (a thermally heated river flowing through the resort), but it is significantly more expensive and busier. Baldi Hot Springs is a more affordable, larger option with multiple pools and a water slide section — good for groups, noisier than Ecotermales.

### La Fortuna Waterfall

A 70-metre cascade into a cold swimming hole, reached by a 15-minute hike down a steep staircase from the car park. The falls are genuinely impressive, the swimming is cold and refreshing, and it is a worthwhile stop if you have time on your second morning before moving to Monteverde. Entry: approximately $18 USD per person. The hike down and back takes about an hour.

### White-Water Rafting — Río Toro

The Río Toro, accessible from La Fortuna, offers Class III–IV white water through a canyon with dramatic basalt walls. Half-day rafting trips are operated by multiple companies in town; Desafío Adventure and Wave Expeditions are reputable operators. Cost: approximately $65–$80 USD per person, including transport and guide. This is a good option for your second day in La Fortuna.

## Days 10–12 (or 11–14): Monteverde

Monteverde is reached from La Fortuna by driving south along the western shore of Lake Arenal — a scenic two-to-three-hour drive with the lake on your left and the Tilarán mountains above. The final stretch into Monteverde involves unpaved roads that can be rough; a vehicle with some clearance makes it more comfortable. The village of Santa Elena is the main service hub, and most accommodation is within a few kilometres.

Monteverde sits at around 1,400–1,600 metres altitude, and the climate is noticeably cooler and frequently misty compared to La Fortuna. Pack a layer. The area protects one of the most significant remaining patches of primary cloud forest in Central America, and it has built an exceptional network of reserves and trails over fifty years of conservation effort.

Three nights here gives you time to explore properly without feeling rushed.

### Where to Stay in Monteverde / Santa Elena

| Option | Price per person per night |
|--------|---------------------------|
| **Budget** — Hostel Monteverde, simple rooms and dorms in Santa Elena, well located for restaurants and the reserves | ~$20–$35 USD |
| **Mid-range** — El Establo Mountain Hotel, large property with excellent volcano views on clear days, pool, and an informative natural history exhibit | ~$75–$120 USD |
| **High-end** — Monteverde Lodge & Gardens, long-established eco-lodge surrounded by private forest, guided walks and birdwatching from the grounds | ~$175–$260 USD |

### Day 1 in Monteverde: Curi-Cancha Reserve

Curi-Cancha is the best introduction to Monteverde's cloud forest. It limits visitor numbers to 50 at any one time — dramatically quieter than the Cloud Forest Reserve — and its 7 kilometres of trail pass through both primary and secondary forest between 1,450 and 1,615 metres altitude.

The recommended circuit combines the Morpho, Leo, Puma, and Alondra trails in a moderate 4.3-kilometre loop with about 200 metres of elevation gain. Allow three to four hours at a comfortable pace. The reserve is excellent for **resplendent quetzals**, particularly during nesting season (January–June), when males display their extraordinary long tail feathers. White-faced capuchin monkeys, coatis, and various species of trogon are regular sightings throughout the year.

Entry: $25 USD per person. Open from 7:00 AM; early arrival is strongly recommended for wildlife.

### Evening: Night Walk at Curi-Cancha

Curi-Cancha runs guided night walks on a 5:40–7:30 PM slot. The forest after dark is a completely different experience — the animals are different, the sounds are different, and your guide's torch reveals creatures that are invisible by day. Common sightings include two- and three-toed sloths (much easier to spot at night), armadillos rooting in the leaf litter, kinkajous moving through the canopy, stick insects, glass frogs on the undersides of leaves, and tarantulas in their burrows. Cost: $35 USD per person. Book through the Curi-Cancha reservation page at reservacuricancha.com/en/tours.

### Day 2 in Monteverde: Monteverde Cloud Forest Reserve

The Monteverde Cloud Forest Reserve is one of Costa Rica's most iconic destinations and is significantly more visited than Curi-Cancha. As of December 2025, the reserve has introduced a new visitor management system with three circuit options operating as one-way routes in pre-booked time slots. Book your slot and circuit online before you arrive.

The best circuit for most visitors combines: **Sendero Bosque Nuboso** (1.9 km, 65 metres of gain) through primary cloud forest with towering old-growth trees to the **La Ventana viewpoint**, looking out over the Continental Divide. From there, **Sendero Chomogo** (1.8 km, 150 metres of gain) climbs to 1,680 metres — the highest point on the trail network — where the forest transitions into the windswept elfin forest of the Continental Divide, and on clear mornings you can see both the Pacific and Caribbean lowlands. The return follows Sendero Roble, Sendero Chomogo, and Sendero Tosi along the canopy ridge.

Allow four to five hours for this circuit. The forest is frequently in cloud, which is part of the experience — bromeliads and mosses everywhere, low visibility in the canopy, and the strange quiet of a forest that lives in the mist. Quetzals are possible here as well.

Entry: $29 USD per person. Book online at cloudforestmonteverde.com.

**⭐ Tip:** Go early — the reserve opens at 7:00 AM and wildlife is more active in the first two hours. Afternoon slots often have lower cloud visibility.

### Day 3 in Monteverde: Optional Activities

With a third day, several additional experiences are worth considering:

**Coffee tour** — several farms around Santa Elena offer half-day coffee tours showing the full process from cherry to cup. Café Don Guillermo and El Trapiche are well-regarded. Cost: approximately $35–$45 USD per person.

**Canopy zip-line** — Monteverde's zip-line operations pioneered the form in Costa Rica; Original Canopy Tour and Extremo Park are both established operators. Cost: approximately $45–$65 USD per person.

**Butterfly and hummingbird garden** — a smaller but excellent stop for anyone interested in insects, with multiple chambers housing different butterfly species and a hummingbird feeder attracting dozens of species at close range. Entry: approximately $15 USD per person.

**Horseback riding** — several operators offer rides through forest and along ridgelines with views toward the Gulf of Nicoya. Cost: approximately $50–$70 USD per person for a two-to-three-hour ride.

## Final Day: Monteverde to San José

The drive from Monteverde back to San José takes approximately three to four hours, depending on conditions on the unpaved road out of town. The paved highway back via Route 1 is straightforward from Sardinal. Allow extra time if you have an afternoon or evening flight; the road out of Monteverde can slow you down considerably.

## Recommended Additions

A few experiences that didn't fit neatly into the day-by-day but are worth considering:

**Mangrove kayak tour, Tortuguero** — some operators offer longer kayak excursions into the mangrove sections of the canal system beyond the main village. This is distinct from the jungle canal kayaking and rewards patience with different bird species and the chance to see roosting colonies.

**Hanging Bridges, Monteverde** — the Mistico Arenal Hanging Bridges Park near La Fortuna offers a self-guided walk across a series of suspension bridges through forest canopy at varying heights, with good wildlife viewing from above. Cost: approximately $28 USD per person. This is a good option if you want canopy access without zip-lining.

**Gandoca-Manzanillo extended hike** — the coastal trail from Manzanillo village extends further southeast into the Gandoca-Manzanillo refuge than most guided tours go. If you have a full day in the Puerto Viejo section, walking the longer trail with a local guide reveals beach, mangrove, and forest habitats in sequence and is significantly more remote than the Cahuita coastal trail.

**Boat tour on Lake Arenal** — the lake west of La Fortuna is large enough to be interesting by boat, and clear days offer unobstructed views of the Arenal cone from the water. A few operators offer sunset tours. Cost: approximately $40–$60 USD per person.

## Practical Notes

### Driving and Roads

Costa Rica's roads vary widely. The route from San José to La Pavona, and the Route 36 coastal road south of Limón, are paved and in reasonable condition. The access road to Juan Castro Blanco National Park, the unpaved sections near Monteverde, and some routes around Tortuguero's outskirts require care. A standard AWD vehicle handles everything on this route comfortably; a 4x4 is not necessary but provides peace of mind in wet conditions.

Driving times in Costa Rica are almost always longer than they look on a map. Build buffer into every travel day.

### Best Time to Visit

The Caribbean coast (Tortuguero, Puerto Viejo) is wettest from June through August but remains green and lush year-round. The highland sections (Monteverde, Arenal) are drier from December through April but remain visitable year-round.

Sea turtle nesting at Tortuguero peaks from July through October (green turtles). Leatherback turtles are present from March through July. If turtle watching is the primary reason you are going to Tortuguero, plan around these windows.

Quetzal sightings in Monteverde and Curi-Cancha are most reliable from January through June during nesting season.

### Budget Estimate

A rough estimate for 12 days, based on mid-range accommodation and a mix of paid activities, excluding car rental:

| Category | Estimated cost per person |
|----------|--------------------------|
| Accommodation (11 nights, mid-range) | $800–$1,100 USD |
| Food & drink | $350–$500 USD |
| Activities & entry fees | $400–$600 USD |
| Boat transfers (Tortuguero) | $30–$50 USD |
| **Estimated total** | **$1,600–$2,250 USD** |

Car rental adds approximately $50–$90 USD per day for a standard AWD vehicle. Travelling on a strict budget (hostels, self-catering where possible, selective activities) can bring total costs to around $70–$90 USD per person per day.

---

Costa Rica's Caribbean coast is one of those places that genuinely surprises people. The assumption is that Costa Rica is a Pacific country — surf beaches, dry forest, and resort zones. The Caribbean side is wetter, wilder, and much less visited, and Tortuguero in particular is the kind of place you think about for years afterward. Paired with the volcanic highlands and Monteverde's cloud forest, this is the most complete version of Costa Rica you can see in a single trip.

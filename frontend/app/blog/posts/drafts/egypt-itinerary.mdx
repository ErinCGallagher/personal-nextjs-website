Egypt was one of the most rewarding trips we've ever taken, and also one of the most confusing to plan. There's no shortage of information online, but a lot of it is contradictory, overly cautious, or written by people who did a package tour and saw Egypt through the window of a bus. This guide is based on a trip we planned ourselves, half independently and half on a Nile river cruise, and covers everything we wish we'd known before booking.

For a deep dive into which temples and archaeological sites are worth your time, see our [ranked guide to Egypt's ancient sites](/blog/egypt-monuments-ranked).

## Arriving in Egypt

Egypt has a straightforward visa-on-arrival process for most Western passport holders. At the airport, you'll find a bank counter before immigration where you purchase a visa sticker: **bring $30 USD in cash**. The fee is fixed and they will not accept other currencies or cards. Buy the sticker, stick it in your passport, then join the immigration queue. The whole process takes about 15 minutes if you're prepared.

Cairo International Airport (CAI) is the main entry point. Giza is about an hour's drive from the airport depending on traffic, and Cairo traffic can be serious, so factor this in if you have an early activity planned the next morning.

## Is Egypt Safe?

The short answer is yes, and more emphatically than most people expect. Egypt is a heavily touristed country with a significant economic interest in keeping visitors safe, and the tourist sites are well-patrolled. We never felt unsafe, not in Cairo, not in Luxor, not in Aswan, not walking around after dark in any of these cities.

The standard travel precautions apply: be aware of your surroundings, don't flash expensive gear, and use reputable transport. Aggressive touts at tourist sites are a more realistic nuisance than actual danger. A firm "no thank you" and keep walking.

**Practical notes:**

- Download a local SIM or eSIM before you arrive. Connectivity helps enormously for navigation and transport apps.
- Uber operates in Cairo and works well. In Luxor and Aswan, your hotel can arrange drivers or your cruise will handle transfers.
- Dress modestly when visiting temples and mosques, particularly for women. A light scarf or shawl is worth packing.

## Finding a Good Guide

This is worth spending time on. "Egyptologist" is not a protected or regulated title in Egypt: anyone can call themselves one regardless of their training or knowledge. The fact that someone has a university degree does not automatically make them a great guide; we met guides with credentials who were dull and surface-level, and guides without formal qualifications who were exceptional.

What actually makes a great guide is the ability to tell compelling stories, read hieroglyphs in context, connect sites to each other, and translate the abstract history into something you can actually feel standing inside a 3,500-year-old tomb.

**Our recommendation:** We would highly recommend our guide **Mostafa El Hefny** for the Luxor–Aswan portion of your trip. He is one of the best guides we've ever had anywhere in the world: a genuine Egyptologist, a great storyteller, passionate about the history, and one of the few guides we encountered who can actually read hieroglyphs, which adds an enormous amount to the experience. You can contact him on WhatsApp at **+20 106 132 6028**.

For the Giza portion, guides can be booked through your accommodation or through the site. Ask specifically whether your guide can read hieroglyphs, and ask for a reference from a previous English-speaking traveller.

## Picking a Nile Cruise

The Nile cruise is the cornerstone of any Egypt trip and getting this decision right matters. There are dozens of cruise companies, and the range in quality is significant.

### Which Direction?

There are two main options:

- **Aswan to Luxor (4 days / 3 nights):** Shorter, slightly more relaxed pace.
- **Luxor to Aswan (5 days / 4 nights):** Our pick. You see the same temples either way; the longer option gives you an extra day and a slightly less rushed itinerary. Crucially, you start in Luxor, which is the richer temple city, and end in Aswan, which is the quieter and more beautiful city to finish in.

Honestly, the direction doesn't matter as much as the marketing tends to suggest. The temples are the same either way, the Nile looks the same from both directions, and the pace difference between the two is marginal. The main practical reason to prefer Luxor to Aswan is that if you're planning to continue to Abu Simbel afterward, you're already in the right city. Choose based on your overall itinerary, not the direction of travel.

### Who We Used

We booked through **Nile Cruisen** and sailed on the **MS Miriam**. We'd recommend them without hesitation. One important thing to know: you may find yourself three or four travel agencies deep by the time you actually book. This is completely normal in the Egyptian travel industry. Nile Cruisen operates smoothly despite this, and communication was good throughout.

[MS Miriam on Nile Cruisen](https://nilecruisen.com/cruise/ms-miriam-nile-cruise/)

### Other Options

**Viking River Cruises** is the most well-known Western option and popular with North American travellers. It is considerably more expensive but comes with the reassurance of a familiar brand and a highly structured itinerary. If budget is less of a concern and you'd prefer a more standardised experience, Viking is a solid choice.

Whatever company you use, ask specifically:

- What's included in the price (meals, excursions, airport transfers)?
- How many other ships will be docked at each temple at the same time?
- What is the guide-to-passenger ratio on excursions?

## Alternatives to the Cruise

The Nile cruise is not the only way to travel between Luxor and Aswan. If the cruise doesn't fit your budget or itinerary, there are two straightforward alternatives.

**Train:** Egypt has a reliable overnight sleeper train between Luxor and Aswan that is comfortable, affordable, and genuinely pleasant. The journey takes around three to four hours. Foreign tourists are generally directed to purchase tickets through an official booking office or their hotel rather than at the counter, but it is straightforward. The train passes through the Nile Valley and the scenery is good. You'll need to arrange your own excursions to the temples independently, but this gives you more flexibility and a meaningfully lower price point.

**Flight:** EgyptAir operates direct flights between Luxor (LXE) and Aswan (ASW), around 45 minutes in the air. It's the fastest option and not expensive by Western standards, though it obviously means you skip the Nile Valley entirely and miss Edfu and Kom Ombo. Best suited if your time is very tight and you've already decided to skip those temples.

**Private taxi/driver:** Hiring a private driver for the Luxor to Aswan run is a popular alternative that gives you the most control. The drive is roughly three to four hours along the Nile and the road is good. Drivers can stop at Edfu and Kom Ombo temples en route, covering the same ground as the cruise excursions at your own pace. Negotiate the route and price upfront. Your hotel in Luxor can arrange this, or your guide can recommend someone reliable. Expect to pay in the range of $60–100 USD for the full transfer with temple stops, though prices vary.

If you're travelling independently and want to see Edfu and Kom Ombo without a cruise, both are doable as day trips. **Kom Ombo** is close enough to Aswan (around 45 minutes) to make an easy half-day trip from there. **Edfu** is roughly midway between the two cities and can be reached as a day trip from either Luxor or Aswan, though it's a longer drive from Aswan. One practical note: Edfu and Kom Ombo sit on opposite sides of the Nile, and there are no bridges between Luxor and Aswan to switch banks. Factor this into your planning if you're trying to visit both on the same day — it's easier to treat them as separate trips from your base city.

Either alternative pairs well with booking accommodation independently in Aswan rather than relying on the cruise ship for those nights.

## Main Cities

### Giza / Cairo

This is your likely entry point. Giza is effectively part of the Cairo metro area. You'll need two days minimum: one for the Pyramids complex and one for the Grand Egyptian Museum. These are adjacent and a natural pair. Cairo itself is a vast, chaotic, fascinating city. If you have extra days, the Egyptian Museum in Tahrir Square and the historic Islamic Cairo quarter are worth exploring.

**To get to Luxor from Cairo:** You'll need to fly. There are daily direct flights on EgyptAir from Cairo to Luxor (LXE), typically under an hour. Book in advance; availability can be limited during peak season.

### Luxor

Luxor is the temple city. The east bank holds Karnak and Luxor Temple; the west bank holds the Valley of the Kings, Hatshepsut's temple, and the Colossi of Memnon. This is the densest concentration of ancient sites anywhere in the world, and two to three days is not too much. Most Nile cruises depart from and arrive at Luxor.

### Aswan

Aswan is the most beautiful of the three cities: quieter, cleaner, with a slower pace and stunning views of the Nile. It's where most cruises end. Allow at least a day for the city itself. Philae Temple, the Unfinished Obelisk, and a felucca ride on the Nile are the main draws. From Aswan you can also make the trip to Abu Simbel, either as a very long day trip or (preferably) an overnight.

## When to Go

Egypt's tourist season runs roughly October to April. Outside of that window, the heat becomes the dominant variable: Luxor and Aswan regularly hit 40–45°C in summer, the sites are almost entirely exposed and unshaded, and the experience is genuinely uncomfortable rather than just warm.

We went the last week of April, which turned out to be an excellent time. The season was winding down, which meant noticeably thinner crowds at the major sites — the Valley of the Kings and Karnak were busy, but nothing like the peak-season photos you see. The heat by midday was real and we worked around it (early starts, shaded breaks), but it was manageable. A week or two later and we'd have been fighting the summer.

**Peak season (November–February)** is the most comfortable weather-wise and the most crowded. Christmas and New Year in particular see significant tourist numbers at Giza and Luxor.

**Shoulder season (March–April and October)** is the sweet spot for most travellers: manageable heat, thinner crowds, and full availability of cruises and guides. Late April is pushing the edge of viable but works if you're prepared.

**Avoid May–September** unless heat is genuinely not a concern for you. The archaeological sites offer almost no shade and tours run shorter hours. Ramadan timing shifts each year and affects opening hours and restaurant availability, so check the calendar before booking.

## Day 1: Arrival in Giza

Fly into Cairo International Airport (CAI). Purchase your visa on arrival ($30 USD cash, at the bank counter before immigration). Transfer to accommodation in Giza.

If you arrive early enough, the plateau is visible from many spots in Giza and the view from the road as you approach is a good first taste. Save your energy. The pyramids deserve a full morning, not a jet-lagged afternoon.

We stayed near the plateau to minimise transfer time in the morning.

## Day 2: Pyramids Complex and Grand Egyptian Museum

An early start is non-negotiable. The Pyramids open at 8am and the difference between arriving at 8am and 9am is significant, both in crowd levels and in the heat.

If going inside Khufu's pyramid is on your list: it requires a separate ticket from the main entrance, buy it when you arrive. It's hot, steep, and claustrophobic inside the ascending passage, but the chamber itself is one of the most singular experiences we've had anywhere.

In the afternoon, head to the **Grand Egyptian Museum (GEM)**, minutes from the plateau and a natural pairing. Budget 2–3 hours. The Tutankhamun collection is the highlight. Several good restaurants on site.

**Ticket note:** Buy your GEM tickets online in advance and make sure to select "Other Nationality." [Ticket link](https://tickets.gem.eg/)

## Day 3: Fly to Luxor, Optional Dendera & Abydos Temples

Fly from Cairo to Luxor. This is a good day to keep flexible.

If you have energy: **Dendera Temple** sits between Cairo and Luxor and is widely considered the best-preserved temple in Egypt, with extraordinary surviving colour on its painted ceilings. It's one of our bigger regrets from this trip. You can stop here en route if you coordinate your transfer times carefully, or do it as a day trip from Luxor before your cruise begins.

If you'd rather ease into Luxor: explore the city, visit the **Luxor Museum**, or walk the Avenue of Sphinxes in the evening when it's cooler and lit up.

## Day 4: Start Nile Cruise, Karnak and Luxor Temples

Board your Nile cruise. Most cruises include excursions to Karnak and Luxor Temple on the boarding day.

**Karnak** is the priority. This is not a single temple but an enormous walled city of temples and chapels built and expanded over 2,000 years. In sheltered spots between the columns, vivid original colour survives: reds, blues, and yellows that give you a sense of what these spaces looked like as living religious centres. Give it as much time as your itinerary allows.

**Luxor Temple** is a shorter visit but beautiful in the evening, when it's illuminated and cooler. The recently restored Avenue of Sphinxes running between the two complexes is one of the great ancient processional ways.

## Day 5: West Bank of Luxor

The west bank is where the pharaohs were buried, and it deserves a full day.

**Valley of the Kings** is the centrepiece. Your entry ticket covers three tombs of your choice out of the eight to twelve currently open. Your guide's recommendation here matters; tomb quality varies significantly. We entered KV11, KV8, and KV2 on our guide's suggestion, as they have the best-preserved colour. Tutankhamun (KV62), Seti I (KV17), and Ramses V/VI (KV9) require separate additional tickets.

**Tip:** The site opens at 8am, which is when all the cruise groups arrive. If your itinerary allows a late afternoon visit instead, you'll have near-empty tombs and better light.

**Temple of Hatshepsut** is next: three colonnaded terraces rising against a sheer limestone cliff, unlike anything else built in ancient Egypt. The interior is sparser than Karnak, but the architecture and the story of Hatshepsut herself (one of the few female pharaohs, later erased from history by her successor) make it essential.

**Colossi of Memnon** are a quick stop on the way back: two 18-metre seated statues that are all that remains of what was once the largest mortuary temple in Egypt. Free to visit if you're already on the west bank.

**Optional:** A hot air balloon over the west bank at sunrise is one of the more spectacular ways to see the Theban necropolis. Most cruise operators can arrange this; book the night before.

## Day 6: Edfu and Kom Ombo Temples

The cruise departs Luxor and sails south. Two temple stops en route to Aswan.

**Edfu Temple** is the most structurally complete ancient Egyptian temple still standing. The 36-metre entrance pylons are imposing, and the preserved interior gives a genuine sense of scale. It's also a record of Egypt's complicated later history: tourist graffiti from two centuries, bullet holes left by Napoleon's troops, and sections deliberately defaced by early Christians and later inhabitants.

**Kom Ombo** is more ruined but has a striking position on the Nile bank and an unusual story: it's the only ancient Egyptian temple dedicated equally to two gods simultaneously, with perfectly symmetrical twin sanctuaries. Catching it at sunset from the water is one of the more photogenic moments of the cruise.

## Day 7: Aswan

A morning sail brings the cruise into Aswan. This is the most beautiful stretch of the river: slower, greener, with granite outcroppings in the water and the light completely different from Luxor.

**Philae Temple** is the standout: an island temple complex reached by a short motorboat from the shore, with the water surrounding it on all sides. The whole complex was dismantled and relocated to higher ground in the 1970s before the Aswan High Dam flooded the original island. The engineering story rivals the temple's history.

**Unfinished Obelisk** is a short stop: a massive obelisk that cracked during carving and was abandoned in place. It's the largest known ancient obelisk and gives a very clear picture of how these structures were made.

**Felucca ride** on the Nile is the right way to end the afternoon: a traditional Egyptian sailboat, slow, quiet, and completely different in pace from the rest of the trip.

**Optional:** Your captain can sail you to a **Nubian village** along the west bank. The Nubian people have their own distinct culture, language, and aesthetic; their villages are brightly painted and the welcome is genuine. Worth doing if your schedule allows.

## Day 8: End of Cruise, Optional Abu Simbel

The cruise ends in Aswan. What you do next depends on how much time you have.

**Abu Simbel** is 280km south and genuinely extraordinary: two temples carved directly into a sandstone cliff face, fronted by four colossal statues of Ramesses II over 20 metres tall. The relocation story (the entire facade was cut out and reassembled on higher ground in the 1960s to save it from the Aswan Dam) rivals the site itself. The remoteness keeps crowds low.

You have three options for getting there:

1. **Day trip from Aswan:** Departs at ~4:30am, four-hour drive each way, brief visit, back by evening. Very long day.
2. **Overnight by air:** About an hour, limited flight availability.
3. **Overnight by car (what we did):** Drive from Aswan at your leisure, spend the night, see the sound and light show in the evening (genuinely excellent, 40 minutes, beautifully produced), and tour the temples in the afternoon when the morning crowds are gone.

If Abu Simbel is on your list, the overnight drive is the right call.

## Day 9: Abu Simbel back to Cairo

Drive back to Aswan in the morning, then fly from Aswan to Cairo. You'll likely need to stay a night in Cairo before your international flight the next day. We booked at Le Meridan at the airport for convenience, but there are also good options in downtown Cairo if you want to be closer to the city for your last night.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What currency is used, and should I bring USD?

Egypt uses the Egyptian Pound (EGP). Most day-to-day spending is in EGP, but **you need $30 USD cash** for the visa-on-arrival at Cairo airport, cards and other currencies are not accepted. Many guides, tour operators, and cruise companies invoice in USD for larger payments, so carrying a modest amount of USD is worthwhile beyond just the visa fee. Our guide told us he would have preferred we tipped in USD because Egypt is currently experiencing a lot of inflation and the EGP is losing value.

### Can I use cash or card?

Cards are widely accepted in hotels, restaurants, and shops in Cairo, Luxor, and Aswan, especially those catering to tourists. However, smaller vendors, local markets, and guides prefer and only accept cash. It's a good idea to carry a mix of cash and cards. We withdrew cash 3 times during our trip, around $600 CAD total.

### How do I get cash in Egypt?

Get Egyptian Pounds out at the airport when you arrive. There are ATMs and exchange desks there. If you need more cash during your trip, **CIB (Commercial International Bank) ATMs** are the most reliable option for foreign cards and don't charge withdrawal fees. The ATMS will a combination of 100 and 200 EGP bills, which are the most useful denominations for tipping and small purchases.

### How does tipping work?

Tipping is expected and taken seriously. Temple staff who will offer to take your photos, drivers who carry your bag, hotel staff who fetch something for you: all of these interactions carry an expectation of a small tip. Nothing in Egypt is free so always expect to tip for any service, no matter how small.

**Rough guide:**

- Restaurants: 10-15% if service isn't included
- Tour guides: $10-20 USD per person per day, depending on quality. Your guide will make a big difference to your experience; tip well for a great one.
- Drivers: 50-100 EGP for shorter transfers
- Temple staff photos: 20-50 EGP per interaction

Tipping on the cruise is handled differently: there's typically a shared tipping pool for the crew at the end. Your cruise coordinator will advise on amounts, ours recommended $10-20 USD per person per day at the end of the cruise.

### What should I wear?

Lightweight, breathable, modest clothing. Egypt is hot, conservative, and heavily religious. Covering shoulders and knees when visiting temples and mosques is the standard expectation for both men and women. At major tourist sites like the Pyramids you will see tourists wearing shorts and tank tops, but it's best to err on the side of modesty to avoid unwanted attention and to show respect for local customs. A scarf or shawl is only needed at mosques.

### Can I drink the tap water?

No. Drink bottled water throughout. Staying hydrated in the heat is important; most cruise ships and hotels will provide bottled water freely.

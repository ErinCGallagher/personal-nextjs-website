Machu Picchu is one of those places you've seen in photos your entire life, and nothing quite prepares you for the moment you actually see it stretching out in front of you. We visited in February as part of a larger Peru trip, and despite the rainy season, it was awe-inspiring.

Machu Picchu takes planning. A lot of it. You can't just show up. Tickets sell out months in advance, train seats are limited, and if you don't understand the route system, you might end up on the wrong circuit entirely. This guide covers everything we wish we'd known before going: how to book, how to get there, what it costs, and how to make the most of your time there.

## The Basics: What Is Machu Picchu?

Machu Picchu is a 15th-century Inca citadel sitting at around 2,430 metres (7,970 feet) above sea level in the Cusco Region of Peru. It's a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of the New Seven Wonders of the World. The site itself is enormous, over 200 stone structures spread across a ridge between two mountain peaks, Machu Picchu Mountain and Huayna Picchu. Entry is tightly controlled by the Peruvian government, with daily visitor caps, designated routes, and timed entry windows. This is good news for preservation, but it means you need to plan well in advance.

## When to Book Tickets

**Book in advance.** Seriously. Machu Picchu tickets can sell out the day they are released on the website, ticketmaster style. In 2026 they released each month of the year on subsequent days in January. For example, tickets for February 2026 were released on January 12th, tickets for March 2026 on January 13th, and so on. When we logged on minutes after tickets were released, the 9am timeslot for our date was already full.

**How to book:** Tickets are purchased through the official [Peruvian Ministry of Culture website](https://tuboleto.cultura.pe/llaqta_machupicchu). The site can be a little clunky and occasionally goes down under heavy traffic, so be patient. If it's not loading try a Peru VPN or changing the website to be in Spanish. Both these things resolved our issues. You can also book through authorised third-party sellers, but there's no benefit to doing so. You can also book through a tour operator and they will purchase the tickets for you.

They also sell [1000 tickets](https://www.machupicchu.gob.pe/purchase-of-tickets-in-person) daily at the ticket office in Aguas Calientes. You must arrive early in the morning (before 6am) to try to get one of these. It's not guaranteed, and we wouldn't recommend relying on this method.

**Important:** Your ticket is tied to your passport number and a specific entry time. You cannot transfer it to someone else.

## Routes: Which Circuit Should You Choose?

Machu Picchu is divided into numbered circuits, and you must choose one when buying your ticket. The circuit you select determines which parts of the citadel you see and how you experience the site. There are currently [three main circuits](https://www.machupicchu.gob.pe/circuitos-y-rutas-de-visita/), but the most popular for first-time visitors is Circuit 2A and 2B.

### Route 2B (What We Did — Our Recommendation)

**Circuit 2B** is widely regarded as the best option for first-time visitors and it's the one we chose. It includes the classic panoramic overview of the entire citadel from above, the terraces, the agricultural zone, and the main urban sector.

**Circuit 2A** This has a lot of overlap with 2B, the route just goes one platform below the 2B platform for the photo opportunity. There were not staff checking tickets when the 2A and 2B circuits diverged, so you could have gone to the top terrace if you wanted.

**Pros:** Best overall views of the citadel. Covers the most recognisable and photogenic parts of the site. Good trail for all fitness levels with moderate uphill sections.

**Cons:** More crowded than other circuits, especially at the main viewpoint early in the morning.

**Our experience:** We entered at 8am on the 2B route and had plenty of opportunities to take photos without huge crowds. February is the off season, so they number is visitors is capped at a lower number which we appreciated. We got a beautifully sunny day without clouds, and it took us around 2.5 hours to hike the circuit.

### Alternative Routes

These circuits have options A-D which provide slight modification to the circuit, but I've described the general overview.

**Circuit 1** takes you to the photo platforms from circuit 2B, which is the classic panoramic overview. You do not get to enter into the citadel and walk through the buildings.

**Circuit 3** You do not get the classic panoramic views from the platforms on this trail, but you do get to walk through the lower portion of the citadel. You will get a very comparable panoramic view from the opposite mountain.

**Our recommendation:** If you cannot get 2A or B tickets, we would recommend Circuit 3 (A-D). This is the best compromise for getting to walk through the citadel while still getting a panoramic view.

## Getting There: Trains and Buses

Getting to Machu Picchu involves three stages:

1. Getting to Ollyantetambo by bus or collectivo (~2 hours)
2. Taking the train to Aguas Calientes (also called Machu Picchu Pueblo) (~2 hours)
3. Taking a bus from Aguas Calientes up the mountain to the site entrance (~15 mins)

### The Train: IncaRail vs Peru Rail

There are two train companies operating the route between Ollantaytambo and Aguas Calientes: **IncaRail** and **Peru Rail**. Both are good options, the choice mainly comes down to scheduling.

**IncaRail** is slightly smaller but equally reliable. They operate the Explorer (standard) and the 360° (premium, with panoramic car). IncaRail has a nicer waiting area with free tea and coffee in Ollantaytambo and newer trains. Overall this is a slightly nicer experience and would be our recommendation.

**Peru Rail** is the larger and more established of the two. They operate multiple train classes including the Expedition (economy), Vistadome (panoramic car), and the Hiram Bingham (luxury, all-inclusive). For most travellers, the Expedition or Vistadome is more than sufficient, both have big windows and comfortable seats.

**Cusco to Ollantaytambo**: Both train companies offer tickets that take you directly from Cusco. Although is appears to be a train, it is actually a bus operated by the train company.

**Our experience:** We organized our own transportation to Ollantaytambo and then took the Inca Rail Expedition train from Ollantaytambo to Aguas Calientes and back. The train was comfortable, but a bit delayed because it had to stop multiple times to let trains pass. Most of route is single track so all trains must stop and manually change the rails to pass. We recommend sitting on the left side of the train on the way there, and the right side on the way back for the best views.

Prices vary significantly based on season and how far in advance you book. Always book direct through the official websites: [perurail.com](https://www.perurail.com) or [incarail.com](https://www.incarail.com).

### The Bus from Aguas Calientes to the Ruins

From Aguas Calientes, you have two options to reach the citadel entrance: **take the bus** or **walk**.

The bus takes around 25 minutes and departs from a stop right in the centre of town. Tickets cost approximately $24 USD return per person and can be purchased in advance online [here](TODO) or in queues at the bus stop. We recommend buying your bus tickets online in advance to save time.

Alternatively, you can hike up the Hiram Bingham Road on foot, which takes around 2 hours. It's steep but doable, and it saves you the bus cost. We opted for the bus given our early start and energy conservation for the route, which we don't regret.

## Getting to Ollantaytambo from Cusco

If you're travelling from Cusco to Ollantaytambo (which we strongly recommend as a base), the cheapest and most local way is by **colectivo**, a shared minibus. They depart near the Terminal Terrestre in Cusco and the journey takes about 2 hours. Colectivos run regularly throughout the day and don't require advance booking, just show up and hop on. They cost 15 PEN per person, so it is a super afforable way to travel. You may have to wait up to 30 minutes for the mini bus to fill up, but we only waited 15 minutes.

The slightly more comfortable alternative is a private transfer which costs around 100-130 PEN. If you're carrying heavy luggage or are short on time, this might be worth it.

## How to Get a Guide

Hiring a guide at Machu Picchu is genuinely worth it. The site on its own, without context, is impressive but a little confusing. A guide transforms it — you'll understand what you're looking at, hear the stories behind the Temple of the Sun, the agricultural terraces, and why the Incas chose this specific ridge.

You have two options:

**Book a guide in advance** through your hotel in Aguas Calientes or Ollantaytambo, or through a tour agency in Cusco. Expect to pay around **$25–$50 USD for a half-day guided tour** per person. Private guides are obviously more expensive than joining a small group tour, but the experience is significantly better.

**Hire a guide at the entrance** — there are licensed guides waiting at the Machu Picchu gate who will approach you as you enter. Rates are similar to booking in advance, around **$30–$60 USD for a 2-hour tour** for a small group. Make sure your guide is licensed (they should carry an official ID card). We hired a guide at the entrance and had a great experience — our guide spoke excellent English and took us through the main highlights in about 2.5 hours before we continued exploring on our own.

**Note:** Guides are only licensed to guide within the citadel itself, not on the Sun Gate trail. If you're doing Route 2B, you'll explore the Sun Gate section independently after the guided portion.

## Accommodation

### Aguas Calientes

Aguas Calientes is the most common base for visiting Machu Picchu, as it puts you right at the foot of the mountain. Accommodation ranges from budget hostels to comfortable mid-range hotels. The town itself is small, a little touristy, and built entirely around the ruins industry — but it's functional and convenient.

We stayed one night here before our early morning entry and found it perfectly adequate. The main downside is that Aguas Calientes is a one-purpose town — once you've done the ruins, there's limited reason to stay more than one night. Restaurants are decent but overpriced relative to Cusco.

**Our tip:** If budget is a concern, stay in Ollantaytambo and take the train in and out in a single day. It's a longer day but the savings are significant and Ollantaytambo is a far more charming place to spend the night.

### Ollantaytambo (Our Recommendation)

We loved Ollantaytambo and wished we'd spent longer there. It's a beautiful Inca town in the Sacred Valley with its own impressive ruins right in the centre, cobblestone streets, and a genuinely local atmosphere. It's also the most convenient departure point for trains to Aguas Calientes, which saves you time and money compared to departing from Cusco.

We stayed at a hotel here for two nights (one night before the train, one night after returning from Machu Picchu) and had a great experience. There are plenty of options ranging from hostels to charming boutique guesthouses, most of them costing significantly less than Aguas Calientes equivalents.

## Our Itinerary

This is exactly how we structured our Machu Picchu trip as part of a larger Peru adventure. We used Cusco as our base and did a 4-day loop through the Sacred Valley.

**Day 1 — Arrive in Cusco**

Stayed at an Airbnb in Cusco. Took it slow to acclimatise to the altitude (3,400 metres / 11,150 feet). Explored the main plaza, had dinner, went to bed early. Do not underestimate altitude sickness in Cusco — give yourself at least a full day before doing anything strenuous.

**Day 2 — Cusco → Ollantaytambo → Aguas Calientes**

Took a colectivo from Cusco to Ollantaytambo in the morning (about 1.5 hours, $3–$4 USD per person). Spent the afternoon exploring the Ollantaytambo archaeological site — it's spectacular and often overlooked in favour of Machu Picchu. Caught the Peru Rail Expedition train at 7:30pm, arriving in Aguas Calientes at around 9:30pm. Checked into our hotel, had a late dinner, and prepped our bags for the early morning.

**Day 3 — Machu Picchu (Route 2B) → Back to Ollantaytambo**

Woke at 5am, took the first bus up the mountain (5:30am departure). Entered at 6am on Route 2B. Hiked up to the Sun Gate with our guide for the first two hours, then explored independently. Left the site around 12pm, had lunch in Aguas Calientes, and caught our train back to Ollantaytambo at 2:30pm. Checked back into our hotel and had dinner in town — a much-deserved meal after the day.

**Day 4 — Ollantaytambo → Cusco**

Took a colectivo back to Cusco in the morning. Easy and relaxed day to wind down before continuing the rest of our Peru trip.

**What we'd change:** We'd spend an extra night in Ollantaytambo to give ourselves more time in the Sacred Valley. One afternoon wasn't enough to do it justice.

## Inca Trail Options

Walking into Machu Picchu on foot is an incredible way to arrive — especially as you descend through the Sun Gate and the citadel reveals itself below you. There are several trail options ranging from a 2-hour hike to a 4-day expedition.

**Classic Inca Trail (4 days, 3 nights):** The original and most iconic route. 43 kilometres through cloud forest, mountain passes, and Inca ruins before arriving through the Sun Gate at sunrise on the final day. Permits are extremely limited (only 500 people per day, including guides and porters) and sell out up to 6 months in advance. This is the most physically demanding option with a high altitude pass of 4,215 metres. If this is on your bucket list, plan at least 6 months ahead.

**Short Inca Trail (2 days, 1 night):** Covers the final 12 kilometres of the Classic Inca Trail, including the Sun Gate approach. A much more accessible option that still gives you the iconic sunrise arrival through Inti Punku. Permits are also limited and should be booked well in advance. This is often the sweet spot for travellers who want the trail experience without the full 4-day commitment.

**Salkantay Trek (5 days):** An alternative trekking route that approaches Machu Picchu via the Salkantay Pass (4,600 metres), the highest point on any of the main routes. No permit required, spectacular scenery, and far less crowded than the Inca Trail. This is a popular choice for those who missed out on Inca Trail permits.

**Sun Gate Hike (2–3 hours from inside the citadel):** If you're not doing any of the trails above, you can still hike to the Sun Gate from within the Machu Picchu citadel as part of Route 2B. It's a 45-minute uphill walk from the entrance and absolutely worth doing. This is what we did, and the view looking back down over the ruins from the Sun Gate was one of the highlights of our entire trip.

**Machu Picchu Mountain (3–4 hours):** A steep climb above the citadel to a viewpoint at 3,082 metres. Requires a separate add-on ticket purchased alongside your main entry. The views are dramatic and it's less crowded than Huayna Picchu.

**Huayna Picchu Mountain (2–3 hours):** The iconic pointy peak in the background of every Machu Picchu photo. A thrilling but very steep and narrow climb with incredible views. Tickets are extremely limited (only 400 per day across two slots) and sell out months in advance. If this is something you want to do, prioritise booking these tickets the moment they open.

## When to Visit

### Dry Season (May to October)

**Best for:** Clear skies, photography, trekking

This is peak season, and for good reason. Days are generally sunny with minimal rainfall, and the mountains are at their most photogenic. The ruins are crisp and visible, and if you're doing any of the longer treks, dry trails make a huge difference. June to August is the busiest and most expensive window, particularly during Inti Raymi (the Inca Festival of the Sun) in late June, when Cusco gets extremely crowded.

**Our recommendation:** Visit in May or September for good weather without peak-season crowds and prices.

### Rainy Season (November to April) — When We Went

We visited in **February**, which is firmly in the middle of rainy season, and honestly, we don't regret it. Yes, it rained. Yes, there was cloud and mist. But there's something genuinely magical about Machu Picchu in the clouds — the ruins emerging from swirling mist looked like something from a dream. The site was noticeably less crowded, accommodation was cheaper, and the Sacred Valley was lush and impossibly green.

The downsides are real though: paths can be slippery, views are less reliable, and some mountain trails (particularly Huayna Picchu) can be closed due to weather. You're also more likely to deal with rain gear and soggy shoes all day. Come prepared.

**February specifically** is one of the wettest months. The Inca Trail is completely closed in February for maintenance. If you want to walk in, choose the Salkantay or Sun Gate options instead.

**Shoulder seasons (November and March/April)** offer a nice middle ground — fewer crowds than peak season, better weather than the depths of rainy season, and lower prices than June to August.

## Altitude and Acclimatisation

This is non-negotiable: **spend at least one full day acclimatising in Cusco before doing anything strenuous.** Cusco sits at 3,400 metres (11,150 feet) and altitude sickness is extremely common, even in otherwise fit and healthy people. Symptoms include headaches, nausea, shortness of breath, and fatigue. We both felt sluggish on our first day.

Machu Picchu itself is actually lower than Cusco at 2,430 metres, so by the time you arrive at the ruins you may feel better than you did in the city. But the bus ride up from Aguas Calientes, the hiking within the site, and any mountain trails will still feel noticeably harder than at sea level.

**Tips for acclimatising:**

- Arrive in Cusco 1–2 days before your Machu Picchu visit
- Drink plenty of water and avoid alcohol for the first 24 hours
- Coca tea is widely available and many locals swear by it for altitude sickness
- Sleep is important — don't push through late nights on your first day
- Acetazolamide (Diamox) is available and commonly used, but speak to your doctor before your trip

## What to Pack

Machu Picchu's weather can change extremely quickly, especially in the rainy season. We went from sunshine to heavy rain and back to sunshine twice in a single morning.

- **Waterproof jacket** — non-negotiable, especially in rainy season
- **Broken-in hiking shoes or trail runners** — the paths are uneven stone and can be very slippery when wet. Flip flops and white sneakers are a bad idea.
- **Layers** — it can be cold at 6am when you enter, and warm by 10am
- **Sunscreen and a hat** — at altitude, the UV exposure is intense even on cloudy days
- **Snacks** — food inside the citadel is not allowed, and the café near the entrance is expensive and not great. Stock up in Aguas Calientes before heading up.
- **Cash (Soles)** — for the bus, tipping your guide, and small purchases in Aguas Calientes. Card acceptance is patchy.
- **A small daypack** — large bags are not permitted inside the citadel (max 20 litres)

## Practical Tips

**Book tickets, trains, and buses together in one sitting.** Once you have your entry ticket date confirmed, book the train that same day before the times you want are gone. It's worth blocking out an hour to sort all three in one go.

**Arrive at the gate before your entry time.** The queues to enter can be long, especially for the 6am slot. Aim to be at the gate 20–30 minutes early.

**You cannot re-enter once you leave.** This is a strict rule — once you exit the citadel, your ticket is finished. Plan your time inside accordingly, especially if you want to hike to the Sun Gate or a mountain.

**Silence inside the site** (relative silence, at least). It's a sacred site, and rangers will ask you to keep noise down in certain areas.

**Llamas roam freely throughout the ruins.** They're not pets and don't want to be approached, but they will wander right past you and are wonderful for photos.

**Download offline maps.** Mobile signal is unreliable in Aguas Calientes and non-existent on the mountain. Download Google Maps and your train tickets offline before you go.

## Final Thoughts

Machu Picchu is every bit as extraordinary as the photos suggest. Standing at the Sun Gate as the mist cleared and the full citadel came into view below us — terraces, temples, mountains — was genuinely one of those moments that stops you in your tracks.

The planning is the hard part. Once you've sorted your tickets, train, and accommodation, the trip itself flows remarkably smoothly. The key is not to leave it too late: book 3 months ahead minimum, nail down your train times as soon as your entry tickets are confirmed, and give yourself at least a day in Cusco before you go.

And if you can, base yourself in Ollantaytambo. You'll thank us.

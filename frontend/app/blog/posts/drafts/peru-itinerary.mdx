Peru had been on our list for a long time, and it did not disappoint. We spent 18 days travelling through three distinct regions: the Cusco area and Sacred Valley, Arequipa and the Colca Canyon, and finally Puno on the shores of Lake Titicaca. Each region felt completely different, and we left wishing we had more time.

If you're planning a trip to Machu Picchu, check out our [dedicated guide](/blog/machu-picchu) which covers everything you need to know: how to book tickets, which circuit to choose, how to get there by train, and what it actually costs.

## Rental Car

We rented a car for two separate stretches of the trip: around Cusco and the Sacred Valley, and again around Arequipa. Having a car was invaluable for reaching sites like the Maras Salt Mines and Moray that are difficult to reach by public transport, and for doing the six-hour drive into Colca Canyon at our own pace. Roads in both regions are well-maintained on the main routes but can be narrow and mountainous — take it slow and don't drive at night.

## Altitude

Altitude is the single biggest practical factor to plan around in Peru. Cusco sits at 3,400 metres (11,150 feet) and altitude sickness is common, even in healthy people. Symptoms include headaches, nausea, and fatigue. We strongly recommend:

- Arriving in Cusco at least one full day before any strenuous activities
- Drinking lots of water and avoiding alcohol for the first 24 hours
- Trying coca tea, which is widely available and commonly used locally
- Speaking to your doctor about Acetazolamide (Diamox) before you go

Arequipa is lower at 2,300 metres and generally easier to adjust to, but Cabanaconde at the rim of Colca Canyon is back up above 3,200 metres, so pace yourself when hiking.

## Day 1 — Arrival in Cusco

We flew into Alejandro Velasco Astete International Airport (CUZ) and arrived at 6:10pm. Rather than staying in Cusco immediately — where the altitude hits hard — we took a bus directly to Ollantaytambo in the Sacred Valley, which sits lower at around 2,800 metres. This gave our bodies a gentler first night. We strongly recommend this approach if you're arriving late and have a full day planned the next morning.

We stayed at [CASA CRISTOBAL Centenario](https://www.awin1.com/cread.php?awinmid=6776&awinaffid=2754110&ued=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.booking.com%2Fhotel%2Fpe%2Fcentenario-boutique.en-gb.html) and would recommend it. Although a 10 minute walk to the main square, the rooms were very comfortable and the breakfast was incredible.

## Day 2 — Explore Cusco

We took a bus back up to Cusco to explore the city. Cusco's historic centre is stunning — a dense layer of Spanish colonial architecture built directly on top of Inca stone walls. The main plaza, the Cathedral, and the Qorikancha temple are the main sights. If you're feeling the altitude, take it easy and stick to a slow stroll. This is not a day for rushing.

## Day 3 — Sacred Valley: Chinchero, Maras Moray, and the Salt Mines

We picked up our rental car at 10:30am from Sixt at Cusco Airport and headed into the Sacred Valley for the day.

**Chinchero:** A traditional Andean village above the valley, known for its market and weaving cooperatives. The town square is particularly beautiful, with Inca terracing on the hillside behind it.

**Maras Moray Archaeological Site:** One of the most unusual Inca sites we visited — a series of concentric circular terraces built into a natural depression in the earth. The purpose is still debated, but the most widely accepted theory is that they functioned as an agricultural research station, with each ring maintaining a different microclimate. The geometry is remarkable from above.

**Maras Salt Mines (Salinas de Maras):** Just a short drive from Moray, these terraced salt pools have been in use since pre-Inca times. The water flows down from a salty underground spring and evaporates in the sun, leaving white and pink salt crystals. It's visually striking — there are thousands of small pools cascading down the hillside.

[MOUNTAIN VIEW EXPERIENCE Hotel](https://mountainviewexperience.pe/) was a highlight of our trip to peru. The hotel is perched on a hillside overlooking the Sacred Valley, with panoramic views of the mountains and terraced fields. They even have a llama jacuzi experience which is definitely insta-worthy. We highly recommend it for a relaxing stay in the valley.

## Day 4 — Cusco City Tour: Saqsayhuaman and Q'enqo

Our third travel companion, Susanna, arrived on the 11:20am flight, so we started the morning with a city tour before picking her up from the airport.

The **Cusco Tourist Pass** (70 PEN / ~$30 CAD per person) covers entry to a collection of archaeological sites in and around Cusco, including Saqsayhuaman and Q'enqo. If you're planning to visit more than one or two sites, it's worth picking up.

**Saqsayhuaman:** The massive Inca fortress overlooking Cusco. The stone walls here are some of the most impressive in the entire region — enormous interlocking boulders fitted with no mortar. You can walk up the terraces from central Cusco in about 30-45 minutes, or drive up and then walk. The view over the city is excellent.

**Complejo Arqueológico Q'enqo:** A short drive from Saqsayhuaman, Q'enqo is a carved rock complex used for religious ceremonies. It includes an underground chamber and a large carved limestone outcrop. Less visited than Saqsayhuaman and worth the short detour.

## Day 5 — Sacred Valley: Animal Sanctuary, Pisac, and Huchuy Qosqo

With our rental car and now three of us, we headed back into the Sacred Valley for another full day.

**Cochahuasi Animal Sanctuary:** A rescue centre about 45 minutes from Cusco. The sanctuary rehabilitates Andean condors, spectacled bears, pumas, and other native wildlife. Entry was 25 PEN / ~$10 CAD. It's a small but worthwhile stop, and you're helping support the animals' care.

**Pisac:** The town is known for its large artisan market and the Inca ruins perched high on the ridge above. The ruins at Pisac are among the best in the Sacred Valley — extensive terracing and a well-preserved ceremonial centre. You can hike up or take a taxi to the top and walk down.

**Huchuy Qosqo:** A less-visited Inca site above the valley with sweeping views. Entry was 25 PEN / ~$10 CAD. The site requires a bit of a hike to reach, so wear good shoes.

We returned the rental car at 7pm before dinner.

## Day 6 — Ollantaytambo and the Train to Aguas Calientes

We took a colectivo (15 PEN / ~$7 CAD per person) from Cusco to Ollantaytambo. The journey takes around two hours.

**Sitio Arqueológico Pinkuylluna:** The granary complex built into the cliff face above Ollantaytambo. You can hike up to it in about 20 minutes from the town centre for excellent views down over the ruins and village.

**Sitio Arqueológico Ollantaytambo:** One of the best-preserved Inca sites in the country, with massive ceremonial terraces and a partially built sun temple. Covered by the Cusco Tourist Pass.

We caught the **7:27pm Peru Rail train** from Ollantaytambo to Aguas Calientes (also called Machu Picchu Pueblo), arriving around 9:30pm. Checked in, had dinner, and went to bed early.

## Day 7 — Machu Picchu

Up at 6:30am, first bus at 7:00am, entered the site at 8:00am on Route 2B. We had a sunny morning, which we were very lucky to get in February. Left the citadel around midday and caught the **2:30pm train back to Ollantaytambo** (CA$197 for two people). Stayed overnight in Ollantaytambo before heading back to Cusco the next morning.

## Day 8 — Fly to Arequipa

We left Ollantaytambo between 9:00 and 9:30am, took an Uber to Cusco Airport, and caught our 1:30pm flight to Arequipa. We used the afternoon to do laundry and explore the city centre on foot in the evening.

Arequipa is called the White City — almost all of its historic buildings are built from white sillar volcanic stone, which glows in the late afternoon sun. The main plaza is one of the most beautiful in Peru.

## Day 9 — Explore Arequipa

We picked up our rental car and spent the day exploring Arequipa at our own pace.

**Santa Catalina Monastery:** A city within a city, this working convent was founded in 1579 and covers 20,000 square metres. Nuns lived in near-total isolation here until 1970. The blue and orange streets inside are stunning. Allow at least 2 hours.

**El Misti Volcano:** The iconic snow-capped volcano visible from everywhere in the city. At 5,822 metres it dominates the skyline. Trekking to the summit is possible but requires two days and experience at high altitude.

## Days 10–11 — Drive to Cabanaconde and Colca Canyon

We made the approximately 6-hour drive from Arequipa to Cabanaconde, the small town perched on the rim of Colca Canyon. The drive passes through high-altitude plains and small Andean villages before descending into the canyon region. We recommend breaking it up and stopping in Chivay along the way.

Cabanaconde is more remote than Chivay, which is where most tours stop, but it gives access to the deeper parts of the canyon and far fewer tourists.

## Days 12–13 — Hiking Colca Canyon

We spent two full days hiking in and around the canyon. Colca Canyon is one of the deepest canyons in the world, reaching over 3,400 metres at its deepest point. The classic hike descends to the Oasis at the bottom (about 1,200 metres down) and returns the next morning, but there are many shorter day hike options as well.

**Condors:** The canyon is one of the best places in the world to see Andean condors in the wild. Cruz del Condor viewpoint is the most popular spot, and we watched them glide on thermal currents in the morning without any effort.

## Day 14 — Drive Back to Arequipa, Fly to Cusco

We drove back from Cabanaconde to Arequipa and returned our rental car. Susanna caught her **6pm flight from Arequipa to Cusco**, and then onward home. We stayed overnight in Arequipa.

## Day 15 — Drive to Puno

We drove from Arequipa to Puno on the shores of Lake Titicaca — roughly a four-hour drive through the Andean altiplano. The scenery on this drive is stark and beautiful: wide open plains at over 4,000 metres, grazing alpacas, and small adobe villages.

Puno sits at 3,827 metres, so altitude is again a consideration on arrival.

## Day 16 — Lake Titicaca: Uros Floating Islands

We joined a day tour to the Uros floating islands (CA$20 per person). The Uros people have lived on man-made islands of totora reeds for centuries, originally as a strategy to avoid conflict with the Inca. The islands are entirely constructed and maintained from the reeds, which are layered and replenished continuously. Our guide walked us through how the islands are built, and we met families who still live on them year-round.

We also visited Taquile Island, which has a strong weaving tradition and sweeping views across the lake. At over 3,800 metres and under direct sun, the UV exposure is intense — bring sunscreen.

## Tips

**Altitude first:** Everything else in your planning should come after you've sorted out your acclimatisation strategy. Arriving in Cusco and immediately trying to do strenuous activities is a recipe for a miserable few days.

**Book Machu Picchu early:** Tickets can sell out the day they're released. See our [Machu Picchu guide](/blog/machu-picchu) for the full booking breakdown.

**Cusco Tourist Pass:** At 70 PEN / ~$30 CAD per person, the pass covers multiple archaeological sites in the Cusco region. If you're spending more than a day or two exploring ruins, it's worth picking up.

**Colca Canyon vs Colca Valley tours:** Most tours from Arequipa go to Chivay and the Cruz del Condor viewpoint and return the same day. Staying in Cabanaconde gives you access to a much deeper, quieter canyon experience. We strongly recommend at least one night there.

**Driving in Peru:** The main roads are generally good, but mountain roads can be narrow and winding. Don't drive at night.

**Currency:** Peru uses the Sol (PEN). Cash is widely used outside of Cusco — bring enough Soles before heading into the canyon or to rural areas.

## Final Thoughts

Peru is one of the most varied countries we've travelled in. Within 18 days we visited Inca ruins, cloud forest, high-altitude desert, one of the world's deepest canyons, and the highest navigable lake on Earth. It's an extraordinary amount of landscape and history packed into a relatively compact country.

The Machu Picchu portion takes the most planning, but don't let it overshadow the rest of the trip. Colca Canyon was a genuine highlight, and Lake Titicaca felt like nothing else we'd ever seen.

South Korea consistently surprises people. You come expecting K-pop and street food and leave having added it to your list of the best trips you've ever taken. In seventeen days you can move through four genuinely distinct regions: the dizzying urban energy of Seoul, the dramatic mountain wilderness of Gangwon Province, the ancient hanok streets and food culture of Jeonju, and the coastal city of Busan — two different cities hiding inside one. Sandwiched in between is Gyeongju, a city so dense with UNESCO heritage that it is sometimes called a museum without walls.

This itinerary is built around a one-way journey from Seoul south, using Korea's excellent KTX high-speed rail to move between regions, with a circular detour northeast to Seoraksan National Park before heading south. Most stops get two or three full days, which is the right pace — Korea rewards slowing down into its neighbourhoods more than ticking sights off a list.

## Getting to South Korea

Most international flights arrive at **Incheon International Airport (ICN)**, west of Seoul. The **AREX Express Train** connects to Seoul Station in 43 minutes (~$12 CAD) and to Hongik University Station (Hongdae) in 51 minutes. Taxis and private transfers are convenient if you have heavy luggage; the subway handles everything else.

## Getting Around

**T-Money Card:** Buy one at any convenience store or airport machine on arrival. It works on all Seoul subway lines, intercity buses, city buses, and the Busan metro. Reloadable at any convenience store.

**KTX / SRT (High-Speed Rail):** Korea's train network is fast, punctual, and comfortable. KTX departs from Seoul Station; SRT departs from Suseo Station in south Seoul and is often slightly cheaper. Seoul to Busan: 2h40m. Book through the Korail website or the SRT app. Seats sell out on popular weekend routes.

**Subway:** Seoul and Busan both have excellent metro systems that cover nearly everywhere you'll want to go. **Naver Map** is significantly more reliable than Google Maps for Korean transit — use it throughout.

**Intercity Buses:** Covers routes not well-served by train. The Dong Seoul Bus Terminal connects to Sokcho (for Seoraksan); Busan's Nopo Bus Terminal serves Gyeongju.

## Seoul (Days 1–5)

Seoul is one of the great cities. A 500-year-old palace surrounded by modern towers. Subway stops that drop you into a medieval hanok alley or a rooftop bar depending on which exit you take. Five days is not too many.

### Getting Your Bearings

Seoul's key neighbourhoods for this trip:
- **Jongno / Bukchon / Insadong**: the historical core — palaces, hanok alleys, traditional tea houses
- **Myeongdong**: shopping and street food, tourist-heavy but worth an evening
- **Itaewon**: international restaurants and bars, best explored via the side streets above the main drag
- **Hongdae**: university area, live music, street performers, the best late-night food in Seoul
- **Gwangjang / Euljiro**: local Seoul, excellent independent restaurants and covered markets

Most visitors stay in Jongno or Myeongdong for palace proximity, or Hongdae for evening access. Both are central and well-connected.

### Day 1: Arrive

Recover, get your T-Money card, and let the city come to you. Hongdae is a good first evening — street food stalls near the park, live buskers in the underpass, a relaxed energy that makes orientation easy.

### Day 2: Palaces, Bukchon Hanok Village

**Gyeongbokgung Palace** is the primary palace of the Joseon Dynasty, built in 1395, and still the most important historical site in the country. The **National Folk Museum of Korea** sits inside the grounds and is included with entry ($4 CAD). The changing of the guard ceremony at the main gate runs twice daily.

**Tip:** Free English-language guided tours run at the palace gate at set times — worth timing your arrival around one. A knowledgeable guide transforms what can feel like a large stone complex into something genuinely moving. Several private tour operators also offer combined palace + village experiences if you prefer a full-day guided format.

**Tip:** Rent a **hanbok** (traditional Korean dress) near the entrance for ₩15,000–25,000 (~$15–25 CAD). Wearing one gets you free entry to all five royal palaces in Seoul. Worth doing once.

**Bukchon Hanok Village** — a hillside neighbourhood of over 900 traditional hanok houses between Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung — is best visited mid-morning before the crowds arrive. The alleys are steeper than they look on a map.

**Changdeokgung Palace** is UNESCO-listed and worth the afternoon for its **Secret Garden (Huwon)** — a wooded landscape with pavilions and ponds preserved almost unchanged for four centuries. Access requires a separate ticket (~$8 CAD) and timed entry; book in advance online.

### Day 3: Cheonggyecheon, Gwangjang Market, N Seoul Tower

**Cheonggyecheon Stream** is a 5.8km urban waterway that was buried under a highway until 2005 and is now one of the most pleasant walks in Seoul. Start at the illuminated fountain near Seoul Plaza and walk downstream through stone art installations, murals, and small waterfalls. Best in the late afternoon when the evening light hits the water.

A short walk brings you to **Gwangjang Market**, one of Seoul's oldest covered markets and arguably its best eating experience. The central aisles are packed with stalls selling **bindaetteok** (mung bean pancakes, crispy and deeply savoury), **mayak kimbap** (tiny addictive rice rolls), and various raw beef preparations. Eat at the stalls, not the sit-down restaurants.

**Insadong** is the neighbourhood for traditional craft shops, antique dealers, and tea houses — worth an hour wandering before evening.

At night, take the cable car or hike up Namsan Mountain to **N Seoul Tower** for the city panorama. Seoul spreads for miles in every direction and the view is excellent. The observation deck (~$15 CAD), multiple restaurants, and the famous padlock fence at the top make for a good hour or two.

### Day 4: DMZ

The Demilitarized Zone is a full-day commitment and **must be booked in advance** — entry for foreigners requires a licensed tour company. Tours depart from various Seoul hotels and the Hongdae area, and typically include:

- The **Joint Security Area (JSA)** at Panmunjom — the negotiation complex straddling the border, where you can stand in both countries simultaneously
- The **Third Infiltration Tunnel** — one of four tunnels dug by North Korea under the DMZ, discovered in 1978 and now open to visitors
- **Dora Observatory** for views into North Korea
- **Dorasan Station** — the southernmost railway station in South Korea, built in optimistic anticipation of reunification

It is one of the strangest places you will visit on this trip. The military formality, the geopolitical history, and the surreal stillness of the 4km-wide buffer zone stay with you long after.

Tours typically cost ~$65–85 CAD per person. **Book through a licensed operator:** Panmunjom Travel Center, USO Tours, and reputable Klook operators all run reliable day tours. JSA access restrictions change periodically — confirm what's included when booking.

### Day 5: Lotte World Tower, Lotte World, Hongdae

**Lotte World Tower** in Jamsil is the fifth-tallest building in the world at 555m. The **Seoul Sky Observatory** (floors 117–123) has indoor and glass-floored outdoor decks — bring a clear day. Tickets run around $38 CAD per person. The adjacent **Lotte World** theme park (~$55 CAD) — split between a massive indoor complex and an outdoor island connected by monorail — is worth a half-day if you're in the mood for it. Combination tickets are available.

Spend the evening in **Hongdae**. The neighbourhood around Hongik University is the engine of Seoul's live music and street performance scene. The pedestrian street fills with buskers on weekends; the side streets stay open very late with craft beer bars, pojangmacha tents, and food stalls.

**Itaewon** is a 10-minute subway ride from Hongdae and worth an evening visit for its density of international restaurants. The side streets above the main strip — particularly the Haebangchon (HBC) neighbourhood on the hillside — have more character than the main drag.

## Seoraksan National Park (Days 6–8)

Seoraksan is the finest mountain landscape in Korea — a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve in the far northeast, where dramatic granite peaks rise above dense forest and valleys of temple rooftops. The nearest town is **Sokcho**, a small coastal city with excellent seafood that most travellers underestimate.

### Getting There

**Buses run from Dong Seoul Bus Terminal to Sokcho** in about 2h30m–3h (~$22 CAD one way). Runs regularly; tickets available at the terminal or via mobile booking. From Sokcho Intercity Bus Terminal, the Seoraksan park entrance is a short taxi ride.

### Sokcho

Sokcho rewards an evening or morning beyond transit logistics. **Abai Village** at the northern tip of Cheoksan Lagoon is a neighbourhood of North Korean refugees who settled here after the Korean War. The food is distinctive: **ojingeo-sundae** (squid stuffed with glass noodles), **grilled ojingeo** (squid on stakes over charcoal), and fresh catches from the local fishing boats. Sokcho's seafood market is small and excellent.

### Day 7: Hiking Seoraksan

The park has trails for all fitness levels; these are the two worth anchoring your day around:

**Ulsanbawi Rock:** A cluster of six granite peaks visible from across the park. The trail (6km return, ~600m elevation gain, 3–4 hours) passes through forest before emerging on exposed rock with iron chain sections for the steeper sections. The summit is one of the great views in Korea.

**Seoraksan Cable Car + Gwongeumseong Fortress:** An 8-minute cable car (~$15 CAD return) lifts you to a ridge with the ruins of a Goryeo-era fortress and views that rival Ulsanbawi with a fraction of the effort. A good option for the afternoon after Ulsanbawi, or a standalone if energy is low.

**Biryong Falls and Sinheungsa Temple:** The inner Seorak valley is quieter than the main entrance area. The 4km trail to Biryong Falls passes the 1,000-year-old Sinheungsa Temple on the way in — one of the most atmospheric temple settings in the country.

**Best time for Seoraksan:** Autumn (late September to early November) is peak — the valley turns orange and red. Spring (late April to May) brings cherry blossoms to the lower slopes. Summer is green but crowded. Winter closes the upper trails but the granite peaks against snow are spectacular.

### Day 8: Nami Island and Return to Seoul

**Nami Island (Namiseoem)** is a small crescent-shaped island in the North Han River, made famous internationally by the Korean drama Winter Sonata. The tree-lined paths — particularly the Metasequoia Road and the Field of Dream — are beautiful in spring and autumn and worth two to three hours.

**Getting there:** Take the ITX Chuncheon commuter train from Seoul to Gapyeong Station (~1h20m from Cheongnyangni Station, $7 CAD), then a 10-minute taxi to the ferry pier. The island ferry takes 5 minutes (~$9 CAD return).

**Suggested Day 8 flow:** Early bus from Sokcho → Gapyeong (2h direct) → Nami Island (3 hours) → ITX train back to Seoul → KTX to Jeonju the next morning.

**Chuncheon**, 20 minutes past Gapyeong, is worth a lunch stop if you're returning via that route — the city is famous for **dak-galbi** (spicy stir-fried chicken on a hot iron plate), which was invented here.

## Jeonju (Days 9–10)

Jeonju made a virtue of resisting modernity. The **Jeonju Hanok Village** — over 700 traditional hanok buildings, the largest surviving intact district in Korea — is actually lived in: bakeries, makgeolli bars, craft workshops, and restaurants fill the old buildings. Jeonju is also the birthplace of **bibimbap**, takes its food seriously, and is very affordable. It's one of the highlights of the trip even though most people treat it as an afterthought.

### Getting There

**KTX from Seoul to Jeonju:** 1h40m, approximately $28–35 CAD. Some routes require a transfer at Iksan; others run direct to Jeonju Station. Check when booking.

### Day 9: Hanok Village

Arrive in the afternoon and go straight to the village. The main street can be busy midday, but it quiets in the evening when the day-trippers leave — Jeonju is best for people who stay the night.

**Eat bibimbap properly here.** The Jeonju version is served in a hot stone bowl (dolsot) with a raw egg that cooks on contact, accompanied by a full spread of banchan side dishes. Look for restaurants with queues of Korean diners; avoid anywhere with photos laminated to the outside of the menu.

**Makgeolli bars (sulchip)** are worth an evening. The tradition in Jeonju is free snacks with every round of the milky rice wine — order a bottle, get a Korean pancake; order again, get another dish. It's one of the great food bargains in the country.

Walk the village after dark when it is lit by red lanterns and the day-trip crowds are gone. The architecture alone — curved tiled rooftops, layered silhouettes of buildings stacked against each other on the hillside — is worth the visit.

### Day 10: Markets and Omokdae

**Nambu Traditional Market** is the city's covered market — worth a morning walk for **choco-pies** (a Jeonju-origin snack, better than they sound), rice crackers, dried goods, and a lively fish section early in the morning.

**Omokdae and Imokdae** are two hilltop pavilions above the village that give the best view over the hanok rooftops and the plains to the south. The climb takes about 20 minutes and the view repays it.

Catch the afternoon KTX south to Busan (~$38–48 CAD).

## Busan: Seomyeon and West Busan (Days 11–13)

Busan is Korea's second city and its personality is entirely different from Seoul. Built along a narrow coastal valley with mountains on three sides and the ocean on the fourth, the city's neighbourhoods are all distinct rather than bleeding into one another.

**On the order of the two Busan bases:** Keep the sequence as written — Seomyeon first, then Gyeongju, then Haeundae. Arriving from Jeonju by KTX, you pull into Busan Station in the city centre, a 10-minute subway ride from Seomyeon. Seomyeon gives you the best base for the western attractions covered here. After Gyeongju (which sits northeast of Busan), returning to Haeundae in east Busan is geographically efficient, and finishing the trip at the beach is a much better ending than finishing in Seomyeon's commercial district.

### Day 11: Arrive Seomyeon

Seomyeon is Busan's commercial core — two subway lines intersect here, department stores and basement food halls are everywhere, and the side streets are full of Korean BBQ restaurants, pojangmacha (street food tents), and late-night spots. It is functional, central, and considerably cheaper than Haeundae.

### Day 12: Gamcheon, Jagalchi Market, BIFF Square

**Gamcheon Culture Village** is a hillside neighbourhood of small pastel houses stacked tightly together above the city, originally settled by refugees and now transformed into an open-air art project: mosaic staircases, painted alley murals, artist workshops, and tiny cafes filling the lanes. It is genuinely charming rather than just photogenic. Arrive before noon to beat the tour groups.

From Gamcheon, head down to **Jagalchi Fish Market** on the waterfront — Korea's largest seafood market. The ground floor is a wholesale trading floor; upstairs, haenyeo (female divers) sell live seafood to be cooked on the spot. Point at what you want, agree a price, and it comes up battered and fried or raw with accompaniments. Eating fresh seafood in a styrofoam cup while the market hustles around you is not easily replicated.

**BIFF Square** (Busan International Film Festival Square) in Nampo-dong is a short walk from Jagalchi — director handprints set into the pavement, outdoor screenings during festival season, and good street food stalls. The adjacent **Gukje Market** is Busan's largest traditional market: covered alleys selling everything from vintage military surplus to dried seafood to cheap clothing.

**In the evening:** Consider the subway to **Gwangalli Beach** (~20 minutes from Seomyeon). Gwangalli is quieter than Haeundae but has arguably the best nighttime views in Busan — the twin spans of Gwangan Bridge lit up offshore, with beach bars and restaurants in front of it. Worth an evening even if Haeundae is still to come.

### Day 13: Geumjeong Fortress and Taejongdae

**Geumjeong Mountain Fortress (Geumjeong Sanseong)** is one of Korea's largest stone fortresses, winding across the ridge of Geumjeong Mountain above the north of the city. Most visitors take the **Geumjeong Mountain Cable Car** from the Oncheon-jang subway area to the top, then hike the ridge — the main loop is 7–8km with significant elevation changes. The views of Busan stretching to the sea are outstanding.

**Taejongdae** is a coastal park on the southernmost tip of Yeongdo Island: dramatic sea cliffs, a lighthouse, a coastal walking trail, and the city's best sunset views. A slow circuit train (danubi) runs around the park, or walk the cliff path in about 90 minutes. Arrive 45 minutes before sunset at the far viewpoint, which looks back at the cliffs dropping straight into the Korean Strait.

## Gyeongju (Days 14–15)

Gyeongju was the capital of the Silla Kingdom for nearly a thousand years (57 BCE – 935 CE). The city is so dense with burial mounds, temples, pagodas, and ruins that UNESCO designated the entire historic area a World Heritage Site. It doesn't feel like a museum — it feels like a city that didn't bother knocking things down.

### Getting There

**From Busan:** Bus from Nopo Bus Terminal (1h20m, ~$7 CAD) is often more practical than the KTX given that Singyeongju Station sits 30 minutes by taxi outside the city centre. Check both options when planning.

### Day 14: Bulguksa, Seokguram, Tumuli Park

**Bulguksa Temple** (UNESCO, ~$7 CAD) is the most important Buddhist temple in Korea — built in 528 CE, rebuilt after Japanese invasion, and the site of two of Korea's finest stone staircases and multiple historic pagodas. Allow 2 hours. Situated on a forested hillside, the approach is as good as the temple itself.

**Seokguram Grotto** (~$7 CAD), 4km above Bulguksa by bus or taxi, is equally UNESCO-listed and arguably more affecting: a stone cave housing a perfectly proportioned 8th-century granite Buddha, considered one of the finest examples of Buddhist art in Asia. Entry is now timed and limited — **book in advance** through the official website. The 20-minute walk through forest from the bus stop to the cave is part of the experience.

**Tumuli Park (Daereungwon)** is a park in central Gyeongju containing 23 royal burial mounds — massive grassy domes that look like someone has hidden hills in the middle of the city. You can enter one tomb (Cheonmachong) to see the original burial chamber and artefacts. The park is peaceful at sunrise before the tour groups arrive.

### Day 15: Cheomseongdae, National Museum, Anapji Pond at Night

**Cheomseongdae Observatory** is a 7th-century stone tower used for astronomical observation — the oldest existing observatory in East Asia. Modest in scale but with a sculptural quality that makes it feel genuinely ancient. The surrounding park has several other historic stones scattered across open grassland.

**Gyeongju National Museum** houses an outstanding collection of Silla-era artefacts, including the famous **Emille Bell** (the largest bronze bell in Korea, 3.3 metres tall) and gold crowns from the royal tombs. Free entry. Plan 2 hours.

**Donggung Palace and Wolji Pond (Anapji Pond):** Wait for dark. The 7th-century pleasure garden of the Silla court has been excavated and partially restored, and at night the lit-up pavilions reflected in the pond are genuinely spectacular — the best night photography in Gyeongju by some distance. Do not skip this.

**Cycling Gyeongju:** The historic zone is flat and compact enough to navigate by bicycle. Most sites are within 4km of the centre; bicycle hire is available near Tumuli Park.

## Busan: Haeundae (Days 16–17)

The second chapter of Busan is entirely different from the first. Haeundae is the beach district — a 1.5km strip of sand backed by high-rise hotels, a string of good restaurants, a famous traditional market, and some of Korea's most atmospheric sites just outside the main beach area.

### Day 16: Blue Line Park and Dongbaekseom

The **Haeundae Blue Line Park** is a 4.8km stretch of converted coastal railway track with a sky capsule monorail (~$12 CAD) and glass-floored walking sections looking straight down at the sea. **Book the sky capsule in advance** — it sells out. The walking path below is free and very scenic.

**Dongbaekseom** is a small pine-forested island (connected to the mainland by a causeway) at the west end of Haeundae Beach. The coastal walking path takes about 30 minutes and passes Nurimaru APEC House, a modernist pavilion built for the 2005 summit. Sunset from the island's western rocks, looking back at the Haeundae high-rise skyline, is the right way to start your last stretch of the trip.

### Day 17: Haedong Yonggungsa Temple, Haeundae Market, and the Beach

**Haedong Yonggungsa Temple** is what a Korean seaside temple looks like when it has been done right. Built on rocks at the ocean's edge north of Haeundae, with waves breaking below the main hall and pagodas stepping down toward the water, it is one of the more memorable things you will see in Korea. The 10-minute walk from the bus stop passes through a fish market village worth lingering in.

**Haeundae Traditional Market** is the neighbourhood's local market: tteokbokki, hotteok (sweet seed pancakes), grilled skewers, and local seafood at low prices. Unpretentious and very good.

Spend the afternoon on **Haeundae Beach** itself. The restaurant strip just back from the beach has some of Busan's best seafood — the grilled eel restaurants in the narrow streets east of the main strip are especially good.

**Shinsegae Centum City**, a 10-minute subway ride from Haeundae, holds the record as the world's largest department store — worth a walk-through for the basement food hall alone, which is among the best in Korea.

## Getting Back to Seoul

**Fly from Gimhae International Airport (PUS):** Domestic flights to Incheon take about 1 hour and run frequently throughout the day (~$65–120 CAD depending on when you book). This is the most efficient option for an international departure — the domestic terminal is directly connected to the international terminal at Incheon. Allow 2 hours minimum before your international departure.

**KTX alternative:** Seoul Station is 2h40m from Busan Station — a good option if you want a last night in Seoul before flying out.

**Gimhae Airport from Haeundae:** ~40–50 minutes by subway via Sasang Station, or taxi in a similar time without transfers.

## Practical Tips

### Naver Map vs Google Maps

Google Maps is unreliable for transit in Korea due to data-sharing restrictions between Google and the Korean government. Use **Naver Map** or **Kakao Map** instead — both have English modes and give accurate subway, bus, and walking directions.

### Eating

Korean menus often have pictures, and pointing works fine. Key things to eat: **samgyeopsal** (grilled pork belly, cook-it-yourself), **sundubu jjigae** (soft tofu stew), **tteokbokki** (spicy rice cakes, street food staple), **kimbap** (rice rolls, excellent convenience food), **Korean fried chicken** (very good), **bingsu** (shaved ice dessert, best in summer).

In Sokcho: grilled squid. In Jeonju: bibimbap and makgeolli. In Busan: **dwaeji gukbap** (pork rib rice soup, the local breakfast dish), raw seafood at Jagalchi.

### Drinking

Soju is cheap ($1–3 CAD per bottle at a convenience store), and Korean drinking culture is relaxed and social. Makgeolli (milky rice wine) is served cold and pairs well with Korean pancakes. Convenience stores — GS25, CU, 7-Eleven — are open 24 hours and have excellent ready food, beer, and soju.

### Jimjilbang (Korean Sauna)

A **jimjilbang** is a public bathhouse and sleeping hall — you pay a flat fee (~$12–15 CAD), get a uniform, and have access to various hot and cold baths, sauna rooms, and communal rest areas where people sleep on heated floors. Dragon Hill Spa in Seoul is the most famous; Spaland inside Shinsegae Centum City in Busan is one of the best in the country. Worth doing at least once.

### Best Time to Visit

**Spring (March–May):** Cherry blossoms, festivals, warm weather — peak season. Book accommodation early.

**Autumn (September–November):** Arguably the best time. Mild temperatures, spectacular foliage at Seoraksan, slightly thinner crowds than spring.

**Summer (June–August):** Hot and humid. Haeundae Beach is packed but the energy is high. Heavy monsoon rain in July.

**Winter (December–February):** Cold and dry. Seoul is beautiful in light snow. Seoraksan's upper trails are closed, but lower hikes remain open.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a visa to visit South Korea?

Citizens of Canada, the US, UK, Australia, New Zealand, and most EU countries do not need a visa for stays of up to 90 days. Some nationalities now require a **K-ETA (Korea Electronic Travel Authorisation)** before arrival — check the Korean immigration website when booking, as requirements have changed in recent years.

### Is South Korea safe for tourists?

South Korea has one of the lowest violent crime rates in the world. Tourists can walk most areas at any hour. The main practical hazards are traffic (drivers are assertive) and sunstroke in summer. Standard travel awareness applies.

### How many days do I need in South Korea?

Two weeks is a comfortable minimum to see Seoul, one or two regional stops, and Busan. This 17-day itinerary is ambitious but achievable. For a first visit focused on Seoul and Busan only, 10 days is sufficient.

### What is the best way to get between Seoul and Busan?

KTX from Seoul Station to Busan Station in 2h40m — the most convenient option. SRT from Suseo Station (south Seoul) runs at the same speed and is often cheaper. Domestic flights take similar total time once airport logistics are factored in and are only worth it if departing from Gimhae at the end of a trip.

### Can I use Google Maps in South Korea?

Google Maps is unreliable for transit and walking in Korea due to data restrictions. Use **Naver Map** or **Kakao Map** — both have English modes and are significantly more accurate for Korean transit routing.

### Is Korea good for vegetarians?

Korean food is heavily meat and seafood oriented, and vegetarianism is still uncommon outside major cities. **Temple food (사찰음식)** is traditionally vegan and available at Buddhist temples and specialist restaurants in Seoul and Jeonju. Most vegetable-based Korean dishes are cooked in anchovy stock, which is worth clarifying when ordering.
